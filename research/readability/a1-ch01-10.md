## Top problems (most impact on the reader first, at most 15)

### P1 (high) chapters 1–10, throughout — Operational and Northstar vocabulary used without definition; no glossary
- What the reader experiences: A reader starting Part I meets terms the chapters never define and the book has no glossary for. Some are operations jargon a strong engineer may know only loosely (Sev1/Sev2, lead time, escaped defects, WIP limit, SRE, tier 1). Others are Northstar names that only the case overview explains ("Core", "the release train", "core/customer", "no-action pages", "renewal commitments"). "Sev1" is not defined even on the Northstar overview page.
- Example: ch2:409–410: "Two quarters later the same failure mode returned as INC-421, a Sev1 that a customer reported first. It was one of three recurrences among Orders' seven Sev1 and Sev2 incidents". Others: Sev at ch3:238, ch4:445, ch6:407, ch10:470; "release train" at ch1:437, ch2:214, ch4:152 and 216; "Core" at ch1:439 and ch2:209 ("Core merge requests"); "tier 1" at ch5:466 and ch10:322; "escaped defects" and "lead time" at ch4:214–215; "WIP limit" at ch3:182; "SRE Workbook" at ch3:265.
- Fix: (1) Add a short boxed "Northstar in one paragraph" to the first Case Study the reader meets (ch1:410). It should say what Core is (the shared Java monolith), what the two-week release train is, the six teams, and the week-numbering rule. (2) Define Sev1/Sev2, lead time, escaped defect, and tier 1 in one clause where each first appears. (3) Add a glossary page and link it from how-to-use.md.

### P2 (high) ch2:440–443, ch3:440–443, ch4:378–383, ch5:405–408, ch6:437–439, ch9:443–447, ch10:409–412 — Case studies depend on facts from later quarters and later chapters
- What the reader experiences: These are the first ten chapters, but their cases keep citing decisions the reader cannot know yet: the transformation, week-4 ownership moves, QA engineers redeployed in week 10, Chapter 14's week-16 health check, Chapter 21's permissions extraction, a "ten-day decision rule", "weekly Core releases from week 5". A reader who has not read the first-quarter page cannot tell which details matter for this chapter's lesson.
- Example: ch3:440–442: "Billing receives the largest share of the prevention allocation (25 percent) and two of the seven QA engineers. And core/scheduler, previously unowned, is assigned to Billing in week 4". Also ch9:444–446: "Since the QA engineers moved to the product teams in week 10, Platform Operations is five infrastructure engineers".
- Fix: Apply one rule in every Part I–II case: use only facts from the baseline plus anything the case itself introduces. Where a later fact is essential, add a one-line reminder ("In the director's first quarter, the seven QA engineers were moved into product teams"). Cut ch3:440–443 entirely; it adds a second lesson to a case that already lands. State the week convention once in how-to-use.md and in a one-line case header: week 1 is the director's first week, and quarter 2 is weeks 14–26.

### P3 (high) solutions ch01 X2, ch03 X1, ch07 X2, ch09 A2 — Exercises need figures and facts that their chapters do not give
- What the reader experiences: The worked solutions use a $185,000 fully loaded cost per engineer-year (ch01-solutions:150, ch03-solutions:155, ch07-solutions:164). That figure first appears in the chapters at ch10:221. The ch1 X2 solution also uses $1.64M in service credits and $9M in at-risk renewals, which ch1 never mentions. The ch9 A2 solution rests on "canon" facts: five of seven QA engineers automated cases, and "Constraint 5". A reader who attempts these exercises from the chapter cannot reach the intended answer.
- Example: ch03-solutions:155: "roughly $370,000 at $185,000 per engineer-year".
- Fix: Put the needed figures into each exercise prompt: ch1 X2, ch3 X1 ("assume $185,000 fully loaded per engineer-year"), ch7 X2, and ch9 A2 (the QA transition facts). Remove the word "canon" from solutions.

### P4 (medium) ch9 (whole), ch5:237–238 — The title promises performance management, but the chapter covers only underperformance; a cross-reference points to content that does not exist
- What the reader experiences: ch5:237–238 says "The mechanics of the review itself belong to Chapter 9." Chapter 9 never explains how to write a periodic review, set goals or expectations for the year, run the review cycle, or manage strong performers. A first-time manager facing review season finds nothing on it.
- Example: ch5:237–238, quoted above. Ch9's objectives (lines 38–46) are all about gaps.
- Fix: Add a ch9 section "The regular review cycle" (about 700–900 words) after "What ratings measure". It should cover cadence, writing a review from the ch5 record, rating language, and reviewing strong performers. The alternative is to retitle the chapter "Managing Underperformance" and fix the ch5 pointer.

### P5 (medium) ch8:198–234, 392, 459; ch7:11 — "Compensation" means base salary only; terms are ambiguous; the leveling handoff is broken
- What the reader experiences: The chapter never mentions bonuses, stock or equity grants, or total compensation, which a reader expects in a compensation chapter. "Equity adjustment" (ch8:309, 393) can be read as a stock grant. "Merit budget" and "merit increases" (ch8:392, 459) are never defined. Ch7:11 says "Leveling and compensation belong to Chapter 8", but Chapter 8 never covers choosing a level for a candidate at offer.
- Example: ch8:392–393: "promotions and equity adjustments as lines separate from merit increases".
- Fix: Define "pay equity adjustment" and "merit increase" at first use. Add a short subsection "Total compensation" (300–400 words: what is in scope for an engineering manager, what usually belongs to HR and finance). Add "Leveling at offer" (200–300 words: level from the scorecard evidence, not from the candidate's current title or pay). Alternatively, change ch7:11.

### P6 (medium) ch4:271 — "the Peter Principle risk above" refers to something never introduced
- What the reader experiences: The term appears for the first time in the Trade-offs section, phrased as if it had been explained earlier. It is defined nowhere in chapters 1–10; it appears only in reference annotations (ch4:499, ch8:550).
- Example: "One ladder is simpler and pushes the best engineers into management, with the Peter Principle risk above."
- Fix: Define it once where Benson, Li, and Shue are first cited (ch2:27–31): people are promoted on their performance in the current job until they reach a job they do poorly. Change ch4:271 to point back to ch2.

### P7 (medium) ch1:209, ch1:311, ch4:198, ch7:121, ch10:217/219/380/402/409–411, ch3:233, ch5:263, ch8:238 — Authoring and internal language leaks into reader text
- What the reader experiences: Codes such as "disputed claim D7", "research gap G8", and "research gap G10" mean nothing to a reader and link nowhere. Ch10 uses "canon" as a word ("not a canon figure", "the canon's median", "(canon)" five times). Many cases describe their own provenance: "recorded on the baseline page", "the paging figures are additions recorded for this chapter". These read like notes to the book's maintainers.
- Example: ch10:216–217: "Assume $138,000, near the top of the Engineer band (an assumption, not a canon figure)".
- Fix: Delete the G/D codes, or replace each with a plain phrase ("no research we found covers software teams"). Replace "canon" with "Northstar's baseline data". Cut provenance sentences to "fictional" plus one link.

### P8 (medium) ch6:160–171, ch5:84–91, ch8:127–157, ch10:76–83, ch4:85–142 — Research passages bury the takeaway
- What the reader experiences: These passages walk through studies one after another before stating what the reader should do. In ch6 the reader reads two meta-analyses in about 12 dense lines before the actionable point at line 173. Ch5:84–91 is one sentence of about 90 words listing five findings. Ch8:127–157 runs five studies in a row. Ch10:76–83 summarizes two meta-analyses whose only output is "context matters". Ch4 chains six Larson citations through its conceptual section.
- Example: ch5:85–91: "In a summary for a general audience, he reports that nearly half of employees found their one-on-ones lacking in substance; that the strongest predictor of value was the report's active participation, …"
- Fix: Lead each passage with a one-sentence takeaway, then give the evidence as a compact bulleted list or a table (study, population, finding, strength). Split ch5:84–91 into five bullets. In ch10, shrink lines 76–83 to two sentences.

### P9 (medium) ch5:167, ch6:81, ch6:153, ch7:108, ch7:237, ch8:128, ch9:205 — Statistical notation used without explanation
- What the reader experiences: The target reader has no statistics training. They meet effect sizes "g = 0.43 … g = 0.74" (ch5:167) with no gloss, "d = .41" (ch6:81) explained only as "moderate", plus "variance", "moderated", "standard deviation", and a "credibility interval" (ch7:108) before its explanation at ch7:117–119. Ch7:237 gives an arcsin formula in the middle of the prose.
- Fix: Add a half-page "Reading research numbers" box, either in how-to-use.md or at first use in ch5. It should explain correlation, effect size (small, medium, large), variance explained, and intervals in plain words. Replace g and d values with "a moderate-to-large effect". Move the ch7 formula into a footnote.

### P10 (medium) ch5:256–270, ch3:141–147 and 236–244, ch2:229–232, ch4:220–223, ch10:230–243 — Arithmetic in prose crowds out the point
- What the reader experiences: Long chains of numbers in running text. Ch5:262–270 computes person-hours per news item under two cadences across nine lines, and the point ("content beats frequency") arrives at the end. Ch3:236–244 gives about ten figures in two paragraphs before the table.
- Example: ch5:266–268: "they would yield about 17 items in about 70 person-hours (12 × 30 + 39 × 45 minutes, counted for both people), about 4.1 per item."
- Fix: State the conclusion first. Move the computations into a two-row table (cadence, hours, news items, hours per item), or cut them. In ch3, merge the paging figures into one small table.

### P11 (medium) ch3:236–244, ch6:407–411, ch10:230–232; also ch1/ch2/ch3/ch4/ch9 on Grove; ch1/ch4/ch8/ch10 on staff archetypes — Repetition across chapters
- What the reader experiences: The same Billing paging figures (126 of 180, 54 of 64) are recomputed in three chapters. Grove's managerial-output idea is re-stated in five chapters (ch1:54, ch2:80, ch3:211, ch4:114, ch9:82). The four staff archetypes are re-listed in four chapters (ch1:180–189, ch4:105, ch8:121, ch10:169). Each repeat is short, but a cover-to-cover reader notices it.
- Fix: Keep one full statement in the owning chapter. Elsewhere use a single clause plus a link. Drop the duplicate failure-mode entries in ch4 and ch10, or turn each into a pointer.

### P12 (medium) ch4:41–42, 291–293 — The "diagnose a person's actual level" objective has no method
- What the reader experiences: The objective says the reader will diagnose "the level at which a person is actually operating from their calendar and decisions, independent of their title". The chapter offers only one failure-mode paragraph ("previous-level gravity") and exercise F2. There is no procedure or signal list.
- Fix: Add a small table to "What changes at each step" (about 200 words). For each level, list what decisions only that level makes, the typical horizon of the person's open commitments, and who waits on them. Add a three-step calendar check that reuses the ch1 audit.

### P13 (medium) ch6:203–246, ch6:400–442; ch9 — No example of a hard conversation itself
- What the reader experiences: Ch6 gives a five-part preparation and a flow diagram, then ends its case before the Marcus–Teodora conversation. Ch9 ends with a proposal, never the conversation with Bram. The reader's question, "what do I actually say, and what do I say when it goes badly?", is answered only abstractly (ch6:226–228 has a single line of model wording).
- Fix: In ch6, after the preparation list, add a 250–350 word annotated sample. It should cover an opening, a counter-attack ("management ignored on-call for a year"), and the pause-and-resume move. Ch9 could add a short opening script for the first gap conversation.

### P14 (low) ch2:287–289 — Managing former peers gets four sentences
- What the reader experiences: Many first-time engineering managers are promoted inside their own team. This is the transition's most common emotional difficulty, and it appears only as a short trade-off. The Rafael case raises it, then leaves it to an exercise.
- Fix: Add a ~300-word subsection under "An identity change": what changes with friends on the team, the passed-over candidate, the first conversation, and what not to promise.

### P15 (low) ch1:263 vs ch6:262; ch2:142, ch4:85, ch9:93 — Small navigation inconsistencies
- What the reader experiences: "Sam" is the tech lead in ch1's example and a different senior engineer in ch6's. Some passages are labeled "this book's synthesis", which is not one of the labels on the evidence-labels page (ch2:142, ch4:85, ch9:93). The bold evidence labels appear from ch1 onward, but no chapter links to the page that explains them.
- Fix: Rename one Sam. Use "this book's recommendation" consistently, or add "synthesis" to the label vocabulary. Link the evidence-labels page the first time a label appears in ch1 (line 98 or 232).

## Patterns across your scope
- Undefined ops and case vocabulary: in every chapter (P1). Book-wide rule: define every acronym and Northstar proper noun in a clause at first use in each Part, and keep a glossary.
- Forward-leaning cases: chapters 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 9, 10 cite first-quarter and later-chapter facts (P2). Rule: a Part I–II case uses baseline facts plus its own additions; any later fact gets a one-line reminder or is cut.
- Provenance and authoring meta-text: chapters 1, 3, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10 (P7). Rule: say "fictional" once with one link; no G/D codes, no "canon", no "recorded on … page" sentences.
- Evidence hedging stacked into sentences: almost every research mention carries "read at abstract level", "one firm", "not software". Accurate, but it adds 10–20 words per citation and slows reading (for example ch1:232–249, ch10:79–83, ch7:101–104). Rule: put access level in the References list, keep one short strength label in the prose, and move population caveats into a "Limits" line after a group of studies.
- Research before the point: ch4, ch5, ch6, ch8, ch10 (P8). Rule: the takeaway sentence comes first in every evidence subsection.
- Numbers in prose: ch2, ch3, ch4, ch5, ch10 (P10). Rule: more than three computed numbers in a paragraph go into a table.
- Consistent structure helps: every chapter follows the same 16 sections. The fixed "Enterprise Application" and "CTO Perspective" sections sometimes repeat the Decision Framework at a larger scale with little new content (ch2:328–362, ch5:343–382 are fine; ch4:310–345 is thin). Rule: those sections must add at least one decision that does not exist at team scale.

- Total compensation (bonus, equity grants) and leveling at offer: missing; ch8; 500–700 words (P5).

Belongs in ch7 as two short subsections (600–800 words), or as an explicit pointer if another chapter owns onboarding.

- Managing former peers and the passed-over candidate: thin; ch2; ~300 words (P14).
- Method for diagnosing someone's operating level: missing; ch4; ~200 words plus a table (P12).
- Remote and multi-site management: Northstar has Bogotá engineers (ch5:416–417, ch8:261), but no chapter in scope says how one-on-ones, feedback, or retention differ across sites and time zones. A 200–300 word subsection in ch5 or ch10.

## What works well (keep)
- Every chapter states its mental model in one blockquote and actually builds on it. Ch1 (three jobs), ch2 (fast versus slow feedback loop), ch5 (channel owned by the report), ch9 (prove the expectation and the system first), and ch10 (pressure, event, alternative, binding) are memorable and teachable.
- The delegation level table (ch2:145–151) and the "name the level, never lower it silently" rules are among the most usable pages in the book.
- Ch7's explanation of validity, range restriction, and the 2022/2023 revision is a model of making research usable without overselling it. The "what .42 buys" paragraph turns a coefficient into odds a manager can feel.
- Ch9's Bram example (ch9:244–277) shows diagnosis changing the answer, with data. It is the best example in scope of the case illustrating rather than burying the lesson.
- Honest evidence labeling: claims are consistently marked as research finding, expert opinion, or this book's recommendation, and the book says where evidence is missing (ch5:173–175, ch7:175–180, ch10:191–198). Keep the labels, but shorten the hedges (see Patterns).
- Every metric comes with how it is gamed (ch4:169–175, ch7:351–354, ch10:132–136). This is distinctive and valuable.
- Short Failure Modes entries with "Signal" and "Countermeasure" are skimmable and practical throughout.
- Diagrams are simple mechanisms that read at phone width; the reinforcing-loop diagrams (ch2:296–304, ch3:65–77, ch8:334–342) earn their place.
- Exercises are realistic and well set up. Apart from the missing figures in P3, they are solvable from the chapters.
