## Top problems (most impact on the reader first, at most 15)

### P1 (high) 19–26, passim (39 references to "Chapter 27") — The Part depends on a chapter the reader has not read yet
- What the reader experiences: From Ch 19 on, the reader keeps meeting decisions, allocations, and events "from Chapter 27": the 12 FTE prevention allocation, Billing's 25 percent, the proposed tier 1 minimums, the release working group, the week-9 Sev1. Part V has no introduction page. A reader going in order reaches a pointer to its last chapter in almost every Northstar passage. One chapter even admits it: "Chapter 27's case reports how the quarter ended" (24:461).
- Example: "Against Chapter 27's proposed tier 1 minimums, `components.csv` gives, for the 15 proposed tier 1 components: clear owner 11, runbook 6, complete alerting 3, on-call rotation 12." (20:252–254). "The director pointed to the budget rule ... and to Billing's 25 percent prevention allocation from Chapter 27." (22:427–429)
- Fix: Add a one-page Part V opener before Ch 19: "Northstar's quality quarter at a glance." It should hold the diagnosis (release train as the constraint, detection, ownership), the 12 FTE allocation split by team, the tier definition, the five or six recurring characters with their roles, and a week-by-week timeline (week 3 classification, week 4 ownership moves and quarantine, week 5 weekly train, week 9 Sev1, week 11 migration check). Then replace each "from Chapter 27" with "(see the Part V overview)". The alternative is to move Ch 27's "Finding the constraint" and "Funding the work" sections to the front of the Part.

## Patterns across your scope
- **Forward dependence on the capstone** (P1): every chapter from 19 to 26 assumes Ch 27's quarter. Recommended book-wide rule: a Part whose chapters share one case quarter gets a one-page opener with the timeline, the decisions, and the roles, and chapters refer back to it, never forward.
- **Bare IDs** (incidents, components, register items, gap and dispute codes): Ch 23 and Ch 25 are the worst, and Ch 21's TD-00x items are similar. Rule: no bare ID without a descriptive phrase on first mention in each chapter, and no internal research IDs (G#, D#) in body text.
- **Case retelling**: the week-9 Sev1, the quarantine numbers, and the detection-point split recur across chapters. Rule: each case event and statistic has one owning chapter; others refer to it in one sentence.
- **Notes about the writing process in the reader's text** ("this book read only the abstract", "Northstar additions", "the baseline page keeps the original ratings"). Rule: keep the access level in References; the body shows only the evidence label.
- **Arithmetic in prose**: Ch 19, 22, 23, 24, 25, and 26 each have at least one paragraph of chained calculations. Rule: more than three operations go in a table or a collapsible "derivation" note; the paragraph states the conclusion.
- **Exercises that rely on data files without saying so** (23 F2, 24 X2, 21 A2 relying on Ch 27's allocation). Rule: every exercise either uses only facts in its own chapter or names the file or chapter it needs.
- **Terminology drift**: "escaped defect" against "customer-reported defect", "pull request" against "merge request", "trunk" against "main", "change failure rate" against "change fail rate" (DORA's term), and "tier 1 minimums" against "operational minimums". Rule: add a Part V glossary and use one term each.

## Missing or thin content
- **Part V introduction**: the case context, a one-paragraph explanation of each chapter's role, and the shared terms (Sev levels, tiers, escaped defect, FTE, prevention allocation). It belongs before Ch 19, at roughly 600–800 words.
- **Finding security flaws in your own code (Ch 26)**: the diagram has an "Own code flaws" inflow, but the chapter skips static and dynamic application testing, penetration testing, secure code review, and common authentication and authorization mistakes. A director would expect at least the choices and their costs. Add about 500 words under Deep Conceptual Explanation.
- **Observability in practice (Ch 24)**: the monitoring-versus-observability distinction gets one paragraph, with no guidance on what to instrument (structured logs, traces, request IDs), what it costs, or how a director decides on it. Ch 27 X2 then asks for a decision on a $240,000 observability platform. Add about 400 words.
- **QA role transition (Ch 27, promised by Ch 19)**: see P6. About 400 words.
- **Running the review meeting (Ch 25)**: the Failure Modes section refers to "the facilitator checklist" (25:350), which exists only in the template. Add a short list of facilitator moves in the chapter, or link the template there. About 150 words.
- **Who decides tiers, and what they are (Ch 20 and Ch 24)**: Ch 24 X2 refers to "all eight tier 2 components plus the one tier 3 component", but no chapter lists the tiers or the criterion beyond "order or payment path". Add one sentence and a link to `components.csv`.

## What works well (keep)
- Every chapter opens with a blockquoted mental model that the rest of the chapter actually uses. Ch 21 ("debt is defined by its interest"), Ch 23 ("deploying is moving code; releasing is exposing behavior"), and Ch 24 ("reliability is a budget") are clear enough to remember.
- The evidence is labeled honestly throughout. "No study directly compares" (Ch 19), the self-reported 23 percent (Ch 21), and blameless outcome evidence being unproven (Ch 25) build trust.
- Several tables make the argument faster than prose could: the lead-time decomposition (23:244–251), the SLI-versus-customer-impact table (24:109–113), the seven-part standard (20:86–94), test investment against escape profile (22:254–260), and the capacity model (27:227–233).
- The decision frameworks are concrete, with "fits when / fails when / early warning" columns (19:300–305, 20:292–298, 27:283–289).
- Every metric comes with how it is gamed and a paired measure. This is a consistent, distinctive strength across the Part.
- The case studies include failures and costs: the reverted Jenkins gate (Ch 20), Customer Experience's unregistered upgrade (Ch 21), the late QA announcement (Ch 27), and the night pages from low-traffic burn alerts (Ch 24). This keeps the case believable.
- Ch 27's honest close ("encouraging, not conclusive", with the reasoning about counts built on many events versus few) models the reporting stance the book teaches.
