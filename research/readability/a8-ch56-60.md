## Top problems (most impact on the reader first, at most 15)

### P1 (high) 60-capstone-northstar.md:388–560 — The capstone has no workload budget, no working order, and no clear finish
- What the reader experiences: they are told "Work the stages in order" (388), but the stage dates contradict the numbering. Stage 6 is "draft submitted week 2" (482), which is before stages 2–5 (weeks 3–5). Stage 9 (weeks 47–48) comes before stage 7 (week 48), and stage 8 spans weeks 47–49. The only effort estimate is "several weeks of part-time work" (389–391), for ten artifacts, a register, a hinge review and a two-page memo. That is realistically 60–120 hours, and the chapter gives no size per artifact. "Submit" (558) has no recipient. The finish is a pass/fail consistency check (273–283), with no quality bar for any single artifact, and the solutions file covers only the exercises, not the stages. A reader cannot tell whether a two-page stage 4 is enough or whether the hinge review is good.
- Example: "Plan the whole capstone as several weeks of part-time work; the projects it draws on take 12 to 20 hours each, and here you reuse rather than repeat them."
- Fix: add a "How to run the capstone" box at the top of the Playbook containing (a) the working order as a numbered list (1, then 2–5 in parallel, then 6 as revised, the hinge, then 9, 7, 8, 10), with stage 6's week-2 draft explained; (b) hours and a page limit per stage, for example stage 1 at 6–8 h and 3 pages, the hinge at 4 h, stage 10 at 1 page plus notes; (c) a short path for readers who have not done Projects 3, 5, 7 and 8 (which stages to shrink and how); (d) a per-stage self-assessment rubric of three or four criteria each, or one worked stage as an exemplar in the solutions file; (e) a closing line saying who the submission is for (yourself, a mentor, a study group) and what "done" means.

### P7 (medium) 56-recovering-a-failing-engineering-organization.md:271–279 and 358–365 — Statistics jargon the target reader is not given
- What the reader experiences: "effect sizes from g = 0.43 to g = 0.74", "delta = 0.36", "(k = 106)" (239), "standard deviation of about 3.1 (the square root of 38 × 0.25)" and "exact binomial: 0.13 one-sided, 0.26 two-sided". None of these is explained. The point of the binomial passage (a drop from 23 to 15 could easily be chance) is important, but it is wrapped in three sentences of method.
- Example: "conditional on 38 incidents across the two quarters, an even split would be 19 each with a standard deviation of about 3.1 (the square root of 38 × 0.25), so 15 is about 1.3 standard deviations below even."

### P8 (medium) 59-transformation-under-constraint-and-scaling-without-bureaucracy.md (whole) — The title promises "scaling without bureaucracy"; the chapter delivers subtraction under a budget cut

- Example: the objectives (44–53) cover funding, inventory, removal, capacity versus cash and failure modes. Nothing addresses growth.
- Fix: either retitle the chapter (for example "...and Removing Bureaucracy") or add a section of about 500 words, "Adding process as you grow". ## Patterns across your scope
- **Length against the 5,000-word guide, chapter by chapter:**
  - *Ch 56 (8,200): partly earned.* The triage, two-clocks and leader-decision material carries three real objectives. The padding is the research tour (113–128, 165–171, 261–293: cut about 450), the Halvard statistics (358–365: cut about 80), the Northstar case study (cut about 400) and some overlap between Trade-offs and Failure Modes ("promise binge", "quality without trust" re-state 215–219: cut about 150). Target about 7,000.
  - *Ch 57 (8,250): partly earned.* This is really three chapters, and each core section (two tracks, SCCT, commit-or-escalate, resistance readings) is tight. Cut the Weick/Cook paragraph (121–132: about 100), the voice research numbers (213–227: about 100), the outage arithmetic (368–379: about 120), the CTO section's Northstar paragraphs (529–551: about 200) and the ADR-0001 and Greg Tanaka parts of the case study (606–615, 633–642: about 350). Target about 7,300. Splitting the chapter into crisis communication and upward disagreement plus resistance would be the alternative.
  - *Ch 58 (7,850): mostly earned.* Three distinct jobs (plan a cut, lead survivors, integrate an acquisition), each with a self-contained worked example. Cut the legal section (about 250), the acquisition research bullets (225–249: about 150) and the opening cross-references (about 60). Spend about 300 on the notification-day script (P12). Net target about 7,500.
  - *Ch 59 (6,850): mostly earned, misallocated.* Cut the management-overhead research list (201–223: about 150), the CTO section's Northstar paragraph (about 100) and Northstar shorthand (about 150). Add about 500 on scaling (P8). Net roughly unchanged.
  - *Ch 60 (7,700): the Playbook earns its length; the Deep Conceptual Explanation does not.* The stage instructions (about 2,000 words) are the assignment itself. Sections 93–204 (about 1,400 words) mostly re-credit Rumelt, Meadows, Repenning, Staw, DORA, Baron and Hershey, Berman, Beyer and Skelton, which earlier chapters already teach. Cut to about 600, keeping measurement continuity and outcome bias. Trim the Halvard paragraph in the CTO section (376–384: about 120). Reinvest about 600 in the facts packet, the working order, the time budgets and the rubric (P1, P3).
- **Halvard works; late-book Northstar does not.** Every Halvard example in scope (Payroll, Checkout, Field Operations, Corvane, Platform) introduces its people and numbers in place and can be read cold. Late Northstar passages assume the reader holds weeks 1–49 and Chapters 27–55 in memory. Book-wide rule: every Northstar passage after Part X opens with a two-line "where we are" note (week, who is who with roles, which earlier chapter to read) and names each person with a role at first mention in the chapter.
- **Research before the point.** Several sections list three to five studies with sample sizes before saying what to do (56:261–293, 57:213–227, 58:101–137, 58:216–249, 59:201–223). Rule: state the claim or recommendation first, then support it with at most two studies in prose. Put the rest in a short table or in References.
- **Meta-language aimed at maintainers** ("the canon", "idea level", "abstract level", "not read for this book"): see P6. Rule: chapters carry evidence labels only.
- **Three-topic titles drive the length.** Each Part XIII title names two or three topics, and each chapter runs every contract section across all of them. Rule: when a chapter holds more than one topic, let the Decision Framework, Trade-offs and Failure Modes sections be grouped by topic with subheads (Ch 57 partly does this). Readers can then skip, and duplication across topics becomes visible.
- **Strong, consistent devices.** Every chapter has a mental model with a loop diagram, failure modes with "early sign" lines, and exercises marked hypothetical where they go beyond the case. Keep these book-wide.

## Missing or thin content
- **Capstone: running instructions** (P1): a working order, hours and page limits per stage, a short path, a rubric or one exemplar stage, and a definition of "done". Goes at the top of 60's Practical Playbook. About 400 words, plus a rubric in the solutions file.
- **Capstone: facts packet and glossary** (P2, P3): a table of input documents with availability date, location and first permitted stage, and a glossary of horizon, estate currency, stress rule and seat options. Goes before Stage 1 and before the Engineering Organization Example table. About 400 words.
- **Capstone: a learning-experience verdict.** A determined reader who has done Projects 3, 5, 7 and 8 could work through it: every stage says what to produce, what to reuse and what to hand forward, and the integration checks are a real finish line for consistency. A reader arriving after only the chapters would stall at stage 7, which needs Part XII documents with no links, and would not know when any one artifact is good enough. The workload (about 10 artifacts) is realistic only for a multi-week study group or development assignment, which 60:357–359 suggests but the opening does not say.
- **Ch 59: scaling without bureaucracy** (P8). How to add process in a growing organization without accumulation. New section after "Why process accumulates", about 500 words, plus one objective.
- **Ch 58: the notification-day conversation and survivors' note** (P12). In the Enterprise Application or Playbook, about 300 words.
- **Ch 58: the leader's own role and limits.** Nothing on what a director does when they disagree with the reduction itself, which links naturally to Ch 57's commit-or-escalate, or on supporting the managers who deliver the news. Two short paragraphs in Trade-offs or the CTO section, about 150 words.
- **Ch 56: realignment worked through.** The chapter teaches turnaround versus realignment, but both examples treat the realignment parts (Time and Attendance, Northstar delivery) in one line each. A short paragraph on what a consultative realignment looks like in practice (who owns the diagnosis, what the leader does differently) would deliver objective 1. About 150 words.
- **Ch 57: what to do before your first crisis if you are not the executive.** The kit in Enterprise Application assumes authority to set policy. One line on what a director without that authority should propose upward would help most readers. About 80 words.
