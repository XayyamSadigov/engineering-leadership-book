## Top problems (most impact on the reader first, at most 15)

### P1 (high) all nine chapters, e.g. 28-architecture-decision-making-and-governance.md:430 — Cast members appear with no role
- What the reader experiences: a reader who skipped earlier chapters meets Victor Lindqvist, Rajiv Menon, Helena Marsh, Priya Raman, Marcus Oyelaran, Elena Sokolova, Tom Becker, Rafael Duarte, Aisha Bello, Teodora Vasquez-Lund, Sunil Varghese, Hannah Lindgren and Mateo Arango Ríos as bare names. Nothing in these chapters says Victor is the CTO, Rajiv the CFO or Helena the CEO. Exercises (29 X2, 30 X2, 32 X2, 34 X1) hinge on knowing who holds which authority.
- Example (28:429–430): "or, when a dispute reached him, by Victor Lindqvist in conversation, unrecorded." Another (32:496): "and Helena Marsh wants your position in writing."
- Fix: give the role at each person's first mention in every chapter ("Victor Lindqvist, the CTO"; "Marcus Oyelaran, Billing's manager"). Add a four-line "Who's who in this case" box at the top of each Case Study, or link a cast table on the Northstar index.

### P2 (high) 34-spans-layers-and-dependency-mapping.md:349–412 — Chapter 34's case depends on Chapter 35, and the same proposal is then told three times
- What the reader experiences: Chapter 34's case evaluates "the year-2 proposal the director drafts in week 44 (Chapter 35)", its "recommended option", "alternative C" and "alternative D", all before the reader has seen them. Chapter 35's case (35:376–412) then presents the proposal as new, and Chapter 36 (36:384–391) describes it a third time. Chapter 34 also points forward to "the Windows Vista study" (34:147) and to "Chapter 35's Halvard" (34:320).
- Example (34:369–370): "The analysis goes into the year-2 proposal the director drafts in week 44 (Chapter 35). Read with this chapter's tools, that proposal's recommended option (...) does three things and leaves three open."
- Fix: end Chapter 34's case at the M1–M3 recount and the layer test using quarter-2 data. Move the "this chapter's test vs. recommended option" table (34:375–381) into Chapter 35's case as "Testing the proposal with Chapter 34's tools". In Chapter 36, replace the full restatement with a three-line summary and a link.

### P3 (high) across scope — Canon shorthand used as if defined

Give every INC number a three-to-six-word gloss. Add a short glossary of case terms to the Northstar index and link it from every Case Study header.

## Patterns across your scope
- **Northstar as a prerequisite rather than an illustration.** The Case Study sections in 32, 34 and 36 assume the reader carries weeks, INC numbers, people, constraints and other chapters' open decisions. Typical references: "Chapter 9's expectations agreement", "Chapter 14's option B", "Chapter 13 frames that decision". Recommended book-wide rule: each Case Study opens with a five-line "where things stand" box (who, what role, which constraint, which dates), and every cross-chapter reference carries a one-clause reminder of what it says.
- **The Example section is doubled up with Northstar.** Six of nine chapters use Northstar in both the Example and the Case Study. Rule: the Example uses a small invented organization to teach the mechanism; Northstar appears once, for the decision.
- **Forward references in the case chain.** Chapter 34 uses Chapter 35's proposal; 32 and 33 rely on Chapter 16 and later chapters' facts. Rule: a case may only rely on content from earlier chapters or the canon pages; forward pointers say "Chapter 35 takes this up", without using its content.
- **Repeated foundations.** Conway, Brooks, Team Topologies, the truck factor and flattening research are re-cited across chapters (P11). Rule: the owner chapter teaches; other chapters get one sentence and a link.
- **Strong arithmetic hygiene, sometimes overdone.** "Check:" lines are excellent in tables (28, 33, 35, 36). Prose arithmetic about another chapter's ratios (32:256) or long chains (34:191–194) should go into a table or be cut.
- **Inconsistent time references.** Weeks 1–3 of the quarter, weeks 14–16, quarter 5, week 53 (P7). Rule: absolute week numbers only.

## Missing or thin content
- **The 500-engineer band (Ch 35).** Halvard is headcount division only. Nothing shows what a platform organization, a principal "technical direction function" or portfolio planning across groups actually does, or what breaks first. It belongs in Ch 35's Engineering Organization Example, about 400 to 600 words, perhaps one worked breakage at Halvard.
- **The 100-engineer band (Ch 35).** Northstar (80 engineers) stands in for 100, and alternative D is the only sketch of managers of managers. A short worked design of a 100-person organization with two groups would deliver objective 2 for that band, about 300 words.
- **Splitting shared data (Ch 31).** The chapter names "data migration, no shared transactions" as costs and notes that Core's modules share one schema, but gives no guidance on taking a table away from shared access, such as views, grants or a dual-write period. It belongs under "Ownership without orphans" or the Decision Framework, about 300 words.
- **Backfilling decision records (Ch 28).** Northstar starts with zero ADRs, yet nothing says how to record the important existing decisions (which ones, how much effort). It belongs in the Practical Playbook as one step, about 100 words.
- **Undoing a reorganization that failed (Ch 36).** Chapter 34 A3 asks whether to "reverse, adjust, or wait", but neither chapter discusses reversal: its cost, signals and how to communicate it. It belongs in Ch 36 Trade-offs or Failure Modes, about 200 words.
- **Platform funding and sizing (Ch 32).** There is nothing on how big a platform team should be relative to the product teams, or on chargeback versus central funding. Ch 35 gives Halvard 14 percent with no rationale. It belongs in Ch 32 Enterprise Application, about 200 words.
- **A navigation aid for Part VII.** Chapters 33–36 form one argument (load and congruence, then spans and links, then design, then change), but no part introduction tells the reader to read them in order or what each adds. A five-line part opener would do.

## What works well (keep)
- Every chapter states its mental model in a blockquote and then reuses it, and the diagrams show real mechanisms: the board-queue loop (28), the critic/hero loop (29) and the congruence loop (33).
- Evidence labeling is honest and helpful. Each chapter says plainly when no study exists (staff roles, team size, reorganizations), which builds trust.
- Chapter 28's generic board example (6.9 arrivals against a capacity of 4) is the model for an Engineering Organization Example: short, invented and transferable.
- Chapter 31's "four decisions hidden in one word" and the ladder of boundary strengths are among the clearest ideas in the book. The Prime Video passage shows how to read evidence at its source.
- Chapter 33's load-kind table (intrinsic, extraneous, germane, each with a removing action) and its decision flow turn a vague concept into choices.
- Chapter 34's "recount" step and Chapter 36's comparison of payback for the smaller alternative against the full reorganization are original, practical tools.
- Failure Modes consistently name a detection signal, not just a description.
- Cases end with open decisions rather than tidy outcomes, which suits the exercises.
