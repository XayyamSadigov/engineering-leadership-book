## Top problems (most impact on the reader first, at most 15)

### P1 (high) docs/parts/04-execution/15-planning-prioritization-and-delivery-methods.md:417 — The capacity-fiction case appears to prove the opposite
- What the reader experiences: The text says engineering sized "the ranked list" at about 560 engineer-weeks and drew a line at 780, and that 17 items "fell above the line". A careful reader asks why anything fell below the line if the whole list fit. Next, the "capacity fiction" bullet (line 426) says real capacity was about 686 engineer-weeks, which still exceeds 560. The reader concludes the plan fit even real capacity, and the bullet's own argument collapses. The real explanation (effort is not completion; waits, growth, and cross-team items) only arrives in Chapter 17 (line 447).
- Example: "Engineering sized the ranked list in t-shirt sizes (rough classes such as S, M, and L), about 560 engineer-weeks. A line was drawn at the assumed capacity of 780 engineer-weeks…"
- Fix: Say that the 17 committed items summed to about 560 engineer-weeks. Then state plainly why that still failed: the 686 also had to absorb unlisted requests and deal support, and summed effort ignores waiting on other teams. Point forward to Chapter 17's "effort is not completion". If capacity fiction is not the main cause here, demote the bullet beneath "cross-team items", which the data supports strongly (8 of 10 misses were cross-team).

### P2 (high) docs/parts/04-execution/17-predictability-and-managing-uncertainty.md:315 — "Cycle-time percentiles (Chapter 16)" points to content that does not exist
- What the reader experiences: The forecasting table sends the reader to Chapter 16 for cycle-time percentiles. Chapter 16 never defines cycle time, work item age, or percentiles of either. Chapter 15 (line 103) lists Kanban's four flow measures (WIP, throughput, work item age, cycle time) without defining two of them. Chapter 16 (line 101) quotes Little writing "throughput times cycle time" and then switches to "lead time" without saying whether the two are the same. A reader trying to set up flow measures cannot tell cycle time from lead time.
- Example: "Single small item | Cycle-time percentiles (Chapter 16) | Treating the 50th percentile as a promise"
- Fix: Add a short subsection to Chapter 16 (about 200 words plus a three-row table) defining throughput, cycle time (start of work to done), and work item age (how long an open item has been in progress). Relate them to the two lead-time senses already in the table at line 71, and show one cycle-time percentile ("85 percent of items finish within 9 days"). Add glossary entries for cycle time and work item age.

### P4 (medium) docs/parts/03-high-performing-teams/11-high-performance-trust-safety-and-accountability.md:76 — Change failure rate is glossed as releases in some chapters and deployments in others

## Patterns across your scope
- **Northstar number density rises in Part IV.** Ch 15, 16, and 18 each have a case section with three or more tables plus arithmetic in prose. Part III (Ch 11–13) mostly states the conclusion first, while Part IV states it last. Book-wide rule: every Northstar evidence block opens with its one-sentence conclusion, carries at most two tables, and moves supporting matrices to the baseline page.
- **Week and quarter conventions mixed.** Ch 15:224/452, Ch 16:407/450, and Ch 17:246 mix them. Rule: absolute week numbers only, with a glossary link in every info box.
- **Same metric, different glosses.** Change failure rate is glossed as releases or deployments (P4). Prevention allocation is "causes of incidents and debt" in Ch 15:294 but "causes of incidents" in Ch 17:261. "Calibration" in Ch 17 has a second meaning in Ch 8, and Ch 17 does not say so. Rule: a single canonical inline gloss for each glossary term, copied verbatim.
- **Forward references used as definitions.** Chapters lean on later chapters for terms they use now: Kanban board and Sprint Goal in Ch 11:269/439 before Ch 15, "lead time" in Ch 13:14 before Ch 16, "debt register" in Ch 15:239, and "refinement" (Ch 16:236/248/283, never defined). Rule: a forward pointer is fine for depth but never replaces a five-word gloss at first use.
- **Long navigation preambles.** The openings of Ch 14, 15, 16, 17, and 18 each spend a paragraph listing which other chapters own neighboring topics before the reader knows what this chapter is about. Rule: keep the opening to the chapter's own promise, and move the "neighbors" list into a single sentence at the end of the opening, or into a margin note.
- **Evidence-hedging phrasing slows the prose.** Phrases like "is known for", "is widely known for arguing", "as the authors report it", and "A secondary account reports that Brooks called it…" (Ch 17:225) recur. They are accurate but stack up, especially in Ch 11 and 13. Rule: hedge once per source, and use the evidence label rather than repeating "is known for" in the sentence.
- **Repeated Northstar status.** "Quarter 1 cut Sev1 and Sev2 incidents from 30 to 21" appears in nearly every chapter, sometimes twice (Ch 16 box and case opener at 403). It is harmless, but the repetition inside a single chapter should go.
- **Minor rendering issue.** Ch 18:370–371 has no blank line before `## CTO Perspective`. Worth checking that it renders as a heading in every output format (the ebook build especially).
- **Canon question that also confuses.** The Ch 14 team table (line 237), set in week 14, shows Platform Operations at 12 engineers. The box above it, and Ch 13:435, say the seven QA engineers moved out in week 10, leaving five. A reader checking the numbers will be confused. Label the table "baseline headcount" or update it.

## Missing or thin content
- **Cycle time, work item age, throughput definitions (Ch 16).** These are the flow measures Ch 15 names and Ch 17 relies on; see P2. Add about 200 words and a small table in "Where lead time accumulates", plus glossary entries.
- **How teams should estimate (Ch 15 or Ch 17).** The chapters argue against single-point estimates and for throughput forecasting, but never address the practices readers actually use: story points, planning poker, t-shirt sizing (mentioned once at Ch 15:419), and the "no estimates" position. Add a 300–400-word subsection in Ch 17 under "What the research says about estimating": what each practice is, what it is good for (conversation about scope), and why throughput forecasting still needs a slicing rule.
- **How to check psychological safety directly (Ch 11).** The chapter rightly prefers behavioral signals, but mentions surveys only in passing (line 223). It never shows what a safety survey asks or how to read it. Add three or four example items paraphrasing the construct (not quoting a copyrighted scale), with the group-size rule and the warning against tying them to goals; about 150 words.
- **A complete worked set of working agreements (Ch 12).** Only two of Data and Analytics' six agreements are shown (line 428). One full list of five to seven strong agreements would make F2 and the playbook concrete, or a link to the charter template if it contains one; about 120 words.
- **Running an interrupt rotation (Ch 16).** The rotation is recommended in Ch 15, 16, and the case, but never described: who rotates, how long, what the rotating person does and does not pick up, and how to keep it from becoming a dumping ground (the failure mode named at line 282). About 150 words in "Unplanned work: visible and classified".
- **A short sample executive answer (Ch 18).** X1 asks for a one-page reply to the CFO. The chapter gives the ingredients (CTO Perspective, playbook step 8) but no example of the translated answer. A six-to-eight-line illustrative excerpt, not about Northstar, would show the register; alternatively, link the executive-update template.

## What works well (keep)
- Every chapter opens its Mental Model with a single quotable sentence, and the rest of the chapter visibly builds on it. Ch 13's "owner, method, deadline, record" and Ch 17's "a date is a distribution" are model examples.
- The "Northstar so far" info boxes with timeline links, and Ch 13's "What you need to know for this case" box, are exactly the right reader aid. Extend the Ch 13 box pattern to the Ch 15 and Ch 16 cases.
- The evidence tables with a "Does not show" column (Ch 11:104, Ch 14:72) teach critical reading better than prose would, and fit the target reader.
- The illustrative non-Northstar examples (Ch 13's decision log, Ch 14's Chicago–Kraków team, Ch 15's outcome roadmap) isolate the lesson cleanly. They are often clearer than the Northstar examples.
- Ch 15's CD3 sequencing table, with the "P3 stays last unless underestimated sixfold" robustness check, is a model of how numbers should serve a decision.
- The Ch 16 value stream map (diagram plus table plus prediction) and the Little's law teaching with its three cautions are excellent.
- The Ch 17 spreadsheet Monte Carlo box makes the method usable on Monday morning.
- The Ch 16 two-senses-of-lead-time table (line 71) is the right fix for a terminology clash. Apply the same treatment to change failure rate.
- Failure modes are consistently named, with signal and countermeasure. Readers can scan and use them.
- The exercises are solvable from the chapters plus the linked baseline page and Northstar overview. Solutions spot-checked: Ch 11 X2, Ch 12 A1, Ch 16 A3, Ch 17 F1, Ch 18 X2 (which needs the $250,000 tooling budget from the overview, as the exercise points out). One gap: Ch 17 F1's solution expects knowledge of Orders taking core/customer in week 4, which appears in Ch 12 and 13 but not in Ch 17.
