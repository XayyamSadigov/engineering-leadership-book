## Top problems (most impact on the reader first, at most 15)

### P1 (high) 55-boards-due-diligence-and-executive-decision-making.md:201 — Deal and legal vocabulary never defined
- What the reader experiences: The chapter's central diligence tool sends every finding to "price, terms, integration plan, or walk away". "Terms" is then given only as legal words the target reader does not know. The Yahoo example adds another, and exercise F2 relies on "strongly copyleft" without explanation. A reader cannot apply the branch "Terms: warranty, indemnity" (line 210) without knowing what those protect against.
- Example: "the terms (specific warranties, indemnities, escrow, a closing condition)" (201). "certain data security incidents were disregarded in deciding whether a material adverse effect had occurred" (234).
- Fix: Add a short "Deal terms in plain words" note before the findings diagram, matching Chapter 52's terms box. Define warranty (seller's promise that a fact is true), indemnity (seller pays if a named problem costs the buyer), escrow (part of the price held back), closing condition, material adverse effect, and copyleft license. About 120 words.

### P2 (high) 52-technology-investment-budgeting-and-financial-literacy.md:299 — Five financial tables in a row bury the three points
- What the reader experiences: Lines 299–389 run the income statement, the engineering spend split, the balance sheet, the cash-flow statement, and the capitalization test back to back, about 60 figures in all. Line 306 packs a six-item cost breakdown into one cell. Several conclusions need arithmetic the reader is not shown. For example, the $27,750 at line 387 is the 15 percent non-capitalizable overhead from line 325, but the text never connects them. A reader new to finance loses the thread before reaching "What Victor takes to Rajiv".
- Example: "Cost of revenue | 40.6 | Support and success 18.0, services staff 8.5, payment and data fees 6.0, production hosting 3.1, amortization of capitalized software 2.1, other 2.9" (306).
- Fix: Open each table with its one-sentence lesson in bold, as the "finding first" pattern does in Ch 50. Keep the income statement, "Where the $20.3M is", and the capitalization test inline. Move the balance sheet and cash-flow statement into a collapsible admonition, or link the finance record page, keeping only the deferred-revenue sentence and the free-cash-flow sentence. At line 387, write "$185,000 × 15 percent = $27,750".

## Patterns across your scope
- **Week-number density.** Every chapter cites a dozen or more week numbers (14, 15, 40–41, 42–50, 53, 54, 56, 60, 64, 70, 78, 104, 156). The reader must convert them mentally, and the timeline is linked only from the status box. Book-wide rule: in chapter prose, use week numbers only for deadlines the case turns on. Describe the past in relative terms ("in the first quarter", "two weeks ago") and keep exact weeks for tables.
- **Derivations deferred to solutions.** Ch 53 (374–375) and Ch 55 (345–346) put the reasoning behind in-chapter numbers in the solutions files. Rule: a number shown in a chapter is derivable from the chapter. Give at least the one-line derivation inline.
- **Repeated status-box sentence.** All six "Northstar so far" boxes open with "Sev1 and Sev2 incidents fell from 30 a quarter to 14 in quarter 3". This orients the reader, but it uses the box's space on the same fact six times. Rule: keep one shared line, then give the two or three facts this chapter actually uses, and add the cast link.
- **Recurring cross-chapter threads re-explained each time.** The continuation's $150,000 tooling offset (51, 52, 53, 55), ADR-0001 (50, 51, 52, 54, 55), the auth-svc exception (53, 55), and "six of the 40 largest renew in quarters 5–8" (51, 53, 55) each get a parenthetical re-definition. This aids readers who read one chapter alone, at a cost in repetition. Rule: one glossary entry per recurring thread, linked at first use, with no inline re-definition beyond a four-word gloss.
- **Inconsistent names for the same thing.** "top-40", "the 40 largest customers", and "the largest 40". "Prevention", "the prevention allocation", and "prevention engineers". "Estate" is used as jargon in 51 and 55 but never defined. Rule: pick one term per concept (for example, "top-40 customers", defined once in the glossary) and define "estate" in the glossary.
- **Legal, finance, and statistics vocabulary is defined unevenly.** Ch 52's "Terms used in this chapter" box is excellent. Ch 53 (material), Ch 54 (standard error, confounded), and Ch 55 (warranty, indemnity, escrow, MAE, copyleft) have no equivalent. Rule: every chapter that uses domain vocabulary from law, finance, or statistics gets a terms box like Ch 52's.

## Missing or thin content
- **Ch 54, data strategy beyond readiness.** The title promises a data strategy, but data appears only as a readiness test for AI. Missing: how a CTO decides data ownership at company level (who owns meaning across Finance, Product, and Data), where the data team sits, platform choices at a strategic level, and data as a product versus a by-product. Belongs in a new Deep Conceptual subsection; roughly 600–800 words.
- **Ch 54, agentic coding tools.** The chapter treats AI as assistants and chat tools. Autonomous agents that open pull requests or run multi-step changes raise different governance questions (permissions, review load, attribution). Add a paragraph to "Governing AI use in engineering"; about 200–300 words.
- **Ch 53, breach response and business continuity.** These are explicitly excluded (235–237). Yet Ch 55 A3 asks the reader to handle a possible key exposure, and the SEC disclosure rule is introduced here. A half-page is needed on what the CTO must have ready: an incident-to-disclosure path, a tabletop exercise, a continuity plan for the build and payment paths. About 300–400 words. Privacy can stay a pointer.
- **Ch 53, vendor risk.** One paragraph (473–476). Suggested additions: what "material vendor" means, and how to weigh concentration versus switching cost. About 200 words.
- **Ch 52, building and defending the budget.** Objective 3 ("build in both views… defend it in variance reviews") gets the two-view table schema but no populated example and no worked variance review. Suggested: a small Northstar year-2 table in both views, and a three-line variance explanation (plan wrong / conditions changed / execution) with one example each. About 400 words.
- **Ch 51, what the strategy document and roadmap look like.** See P10. About 250 words plus one table.
- **Ch 55, a sample board page.** The chapter teaches translating measures but never shows the finished engineering page as a director would see it. F1 asks the reader to write one. Add a one-page mock-up using the exposure table; about 200 words.
- **Ch 50, the CTO–CEO relationship.** Only implicit (expectations in writing in the 90-day plan). A short paragraph on how the CTO and CEO agree what the CTO is for, and how to renegotiate it when the archetype changes, would close the loop with the X2 exercise. About 150–200 words.

## What works well (keep)
- Every chapter follows the same orienting pattern: a mental-model blockquote, a mechanism diagram, and a table explaining each node. The point is stated early and the later sections build on it.
- Ch 52's "Terms used in this chapter" box and its "one set of events, three lenses" framing. This is the model for how to onboard a reader with no finance training.
- Ch 50's "finding first" paragraph before the calendar table (233–235), and the "keep, give, stop" list with named receivers. The advice is concrete and actionable.
- Ch 51's four bins (excellent, adequate, buy, not doing) and seven coherence tests. The genericity test is memorable and usable on Monday.
- Ch 53's mental model ("a risk register is a decision record, not an inventory"), the acceptance-expiry rules, and the appetite lines written in indicator units with a worked example.
- Ch 54's evidence table and "four readings", the order-error decomposition that turns an AI request into non-AI fixes, and the honest pricing of the CFO's backfill question in three scenarios.
- Ch 55's ethics section (the three tests for a misleading page) and the four extra fields for an executive decision record.
- The cases are open-ended ("the reader decides"), and the exercises are largely self-contained. Ch 51 A2 and Ch 54 A3 supply the facts they need inline. A spot check of the solutions found every exercise solvable from the chapter text plus the facts the exercise provides.
- Evidence labels are applied consistently, and the limits of research are stated plainly (alignment research, AI productivity, the risk-matrix critique).
