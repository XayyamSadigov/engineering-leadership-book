## Top problems (most impact on the reader first, at most 15)

### P1 (high) all six chapters, e.g. 44:230, 46:357, 47:479, 48:442, 49:250 — The quarter-4 briefing and cast page are never linked
- What the reader experiences: `case-studies/northstar/quarter-4-briefing.md` exists for exactly this reader ("for readers who start at Parts X–XIII"), but not one of these six chapters links it or `cast.md` (grep finds 0 links in each). The "Northstar so far" boxes link only the timeline. A reader starting at Part X meets Greg Tanaka, Amara Okafor, Helena Marsh, Rajiv Menon, Sofia Alvarez, Keiko Ashworth, Victor Lindqvist, Marcus Oyelaran, Farah Haddad, Yusuf Demir, Tom Becker, and Kwame Mensah. They also meet "the year-2 organization proposal", "alternative D", ADR-0001, and the 87-seat rule, with no single page to orient from.
- Example: 47:482 "In week 44 the director drafted a year-2 organization proposal; Victor Lindqvist (CTO) decides on it in week 46."
- Fix: In every "Northstar so far" box, add one line: "New to Northstar? Read the [quarter 4 briefing](...) and the [cast](...)." Ch 47, 48, and 49 also depend on the open-decisions table, so add a direct link to that table at the start of each case study.

### P2 (high) 48:440–498 — The Northstar workforce case buries its decision under derivations and canon
- What the reader experiences: In about 60 lines the reader gets the following. Seats and engineers are used side by side ("87 seats (80 engineers)") and the difference is never explained. A mix reconstruction depends on an unstated assumption about backfills. A Bogotá-versus-US cost derivation follows. A Mobile-team conditional ("the plan starts at 79 engineers; it must work either way") comes next. The options table has five columns of long text. US salary midpoints are multiplied by "Chapter 8's 1.4 loading assumption". Core's 41 percent line coverage appears. The case ends on "The outside offer a Billing ledger engineer reported in week 20". The actual choice (A–D, and what Rajiv and Victor each want) is hard to see.
- Example: 48:459 "Against Northstar's $185,000 average over 87 people, US-based staff therefore average about $202,000." 48:496 "The outside offer a Billing ledger engineer reported in week 20, and the year-2 proposal, are still open."
- Fix: (1) Explain once that 87 seats means 80 engineers, 6 managers, and the director. (2) Cut the options table to four short columns (option, what it does, cost or saving, main risk) and move the three arithmetic bullets into a collapsible note or the solutions. (3) Give the week-20 offer and the Mobile proposal each a one-clause reminder ("a ledger engineer has an outside offer; if a Mobile team is created, one engineer becomes its manager"), or cut them. (4) End with a two-line summary of the open choice.

### P3 (high) 49:147–173 versus 49:432–446 — The sample page one uses stage numbers and gates before they are defined
- What the reader experiences: The sample asks to "Fund stage 2 … conditional on the stage 1 gate in week 56" and promises "A gate in week 78" with "the rolling incident total on course for at most 40". This comes about 280 lines before the case explains that stage 1 is the already-funded year 1, that week 56 is the first gate after quarter 4's data, and where "40" comes from (Ch 46's counter). A reader naturally assumes stage 1 is the first stage being requested, and the sample reads as if it skips a stage.
- Example: 49:152 "Fund stage 2 of the quality program, "Hold", for quarters 5 and 6 … conditional on the stage 1 gate in week 56."
- Fix: Before the sample, add two sentences: "Northstar treats year 1 as stage 1, already funded; its gate is week 56, once quarter 4's data is in. The continuation asks for stage 2 (quarters 5–6) and offers stage 3 (quarters 7–8) as an option." Add "(Chapter 46's year-2 commitment)" after "at most 40". Alternatively, move the sample after the case's stage table.

### P4 (medium) 47:486–526 — The VP case depends on a draft the chapter does not describe
- What the reader experiences: The options turn on "the week-44 draft" (79 engineers, 7 managers, span of 10) and "Alternative D" (two managers of managers, 77 engineers). The evidence table cites facts by chapter number (Ch 37, 40, 36), and the case leaves the decision dependent on "Victor's week 46 decision". A reader who skipped Ch 35/36 cannot judge option 3 or see why the span is 10.
- Example: 47:491 "Its alternative D adds two managers of managers (77 engineers, 9 managers)."
- Fix: Add a three-line box: what the main proposal changes (Web/Mobile split, a new manager, so 7 managers plus 3 staff reporting to the director) and what alternative D adds. Replace "Source: Chapter 37" with a phrase saying what was measured ("time study, quarter 2"). Link the briefing's open-decisions table.

### P5 (medium) 45:306, 45:338 — "Capacity is not cash" is asserted but never explained, yet it decides investment B
- What the reader experiences: B's benefit counts "1 FTE of unplanned work returned ($185,000 a year)" as money. The Decision Framework then warns against "Counting capacity as cash", in a single table cell. The chapter says salaries are paid whatever you choose (45:69–70), so the reader cannot tell whether B's $185,000 is legitimate, what it is worth, or how a CFO will read it. Ch 49 (49:268–271) later relies on exactly this distinction.
- Example: 45:338 "A proposal that saves cost or capacity | NPV with capacity valued at cost | Whether savings are cash or capacity | Counting capacity as cash".
- Fix: Add about 150 words to "Opportunity cost and sunk cost". Cash savings (credits, cloud, contractors) reduce spend. Returned capacity is worth the cost of delay of the work it unblocks, and valuing it at salary is a convention agreed with finance. Then show B's NPV with cash only and with cash plus capacity, labelled.

### P6 (medium) 49:217–221 — Correlation coefficients with no translation
- What the reader experiences: "strain (.48), turnover intentions (.43), lower job satisfaction (−.57)". The book assumes no statistics training, and nowhere in this scope is a correlation coefficient explained, so the numbers carry no meaning. "not small ones" is the only gloss.
- Example: 49:219 "It found them associated with strain (.48), turnover intentions (.43), lower job satisfaction (−.57), and lower task performance (−.20)".
- Fix: Replace the numbers with words ("moderate-to-strong associations with strain, intent to leave, and lower satisfaction; a weaker one with lower task performance"), or add one sentence on reading r, linked to the glossary.

### P7 (medium) 48:109–116 — The knowledge-loss finding is unreadable
- What the reader experiences: "historical losses from departed developers' files could exceed three times the expected loss. Naming successors reduced expected loss by up to 15 percent." The reader is not told what is "lost" (files whose main authors have left), what "expected loss" is measured against, or what to do differently.
- Example: as quoted.
- Fix: Rewrite in plain terms: "In Chrome and an Avaya project, the share of code whose authors had all left was sometimes several times what a simple turnover model predicted. Deliberately naming successors for at-risk files reduced it modestly." Then link to the action: "protect the few areas where one departure is a tail risk."

## Patterns across your scope
- **Canon-dependent case studies in Part XI.** Ch 47, 48, and 49 each build their case on decisions from other chapters (the week-44 organization proposal, alternative D, the 87-seat rule, the week-44 succession review, ADR-0001, the CI target), and refer back by week number. Recommended book-wide rule: every case study in Parts X–XIII opens with a link to the quarter-4 briefing. Any canon item used in a case gets a one-clause reminder at first use in that chapter.
- **Future facts in past-dated scenes.** Ch 45 (week 31) quotes quarter 3. Ch 46 (week 41) says its figures are those "the director's two-year continuation proposal later carries". Rule: a scene states only what is known at its week. Later facts go in the "Northstar so far" box or are dropped.
- **Arithmetic in prose after a table that already shows it.** 45:310–312, 46:222–225, and 48:473–486 all repeat in prose what the table shows. Rule: one place per computation. A table with a "how computed" column, or a footnote, never both.
- **Long multi-column option tables with sentence cells.** 48:466–471 and 49:432–437. Rule: at most four columns at phone width. Move exit criteria and risks into a list under the table.
- **Statistical results without a reading.** Correlation coefficients (49:219) and Poisson (46:387, handled well). Rule: give every statistic a plain-words reading, or a link to the glossary.
- **Name density in exercises.** Exercises 48 A1/A2 and 46 A1 introduce named people (Farah Haddad, Yusuf Demir, Tom Becker, Marcus Oyelaran) with role glosses but rely on canon (ledger-export contract, "since week 10", the 29 engineer-weeks platform roadmap). They are solvable, but each should say in one clause why the canon fact matters.
- **Consistent terminology, a strength.** "Quality floor", "cost of delay", "engineer-week", "FTE-quarter", "tier 1", and "decision log" are used consistently and glossary-linked across all six chapters. Keep this.

## Missing or thin content
- **Directors: assessment and hiring (Ch 47).** How a VP judges a director and decides to coach, move, or replace; how to hire or promote one. Goes in "Staffing the director team". About 400 words.
- **Demand side of the workforce plan (Ch 48).** The six-part plan lists "Demand" (skills, domains), but neither the Halvard example nor the Northstar case models it: both are supply and mix only. Add a short demand-versus-supply-by-skill table for Halvard (for example, payments and search skills needed against those present). About 250 words plus a table.
- **Growth and recruiting capacity (Ch 48).** Every scenario is flat headcount. A VP also plans growth: recruiter and interviewer throughput, onboarding load, and how many hires a team can absorb per quarter. A short subsection, about 300 words, or a pointer to the hiring chapter if one exists.
- **Cash versus capacity (Ch 45).** See P5. About 150 words plus a two-line comparison.
- **Communicating the program downward (Ch 49).** The chapter covers executives thoroughly, but a multi-year program also needs a story for the engineers and managers doing the work across gates (why stage 2 matters after "we hit the target"). Even a cross-reference to Ch 43 plus two sentences would close the gap.
- **Build versus buy (Ch 45).** An expected engineering investment decision. Ch 47 mentions "major build-or-buy calls" as CTO-owned, but no chapter in scope shows how to frame one in this economic method. A short worked paragraph in Ch 45, or a cross-reference if another chapter owns it.

## What works well (keep)
- **Ch 44's ordering-error example** (44:236–306). A sample of 400 corrections turns a six-feature list into three cheaper, different bets, which proves the chapter's main claim with numbers. The date-origin table (44:216–221) is memorable and immediately usable.
- **Ch 45's discounting primer** (45:163–203). It defines time value, discount factor, annuity factor, and NPV from scratch with one small worked table. This is exactly right for a reader with no finance training. The routing diagram (45:240–251) and the "queueing versus swapping is noise" case finding are excellent lessons.
- **Ch 46's mental model and five-part no.** "Priced variables, written floor, repetition" is stated early and reused throughout. The two tests for floor versus preference (46:132–135) are sharp. The Poisson table in the case makes chance variation concrete without heavy maths.
- **Ch 47's Halvard allocation example** (47:282–342). Rank first, then compute gaps by area, then show "what the org chart would have done". It is a clean demonstration of partition dependence, and the option (c) table makes the trade explicit.
- **Ch 48's stock-and-flow framing and governance tests.** The mental model diagram, the like-for-like versus hold-the-mix tables (48:264–286), the externality test, and "enforce in the path of work" are clear and actionable. The layer decision diagram (48:328–337) is a good tool.
- **Ch 49's sample page one** (once its stage numbering is fixed), the objection-sorting table (49:184–188), and the "transcript test" for constructive politics. Each is concrete and teachable. Greg's declined back-channel offer is a well-chosen illustration.
- **Evidence labelling throughout.** Research versus expert opinion versus this book's recommendation is consistently marked, and the limits of single-company studies are stated without clutter.
