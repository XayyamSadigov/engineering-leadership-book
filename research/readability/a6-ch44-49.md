## Top problems (most impact on the reader first, at most 15)

### P1 (high) docs/parts/10-business-leadership/45-business-models-and-engineering-economics.md:176 — NPV taught without discounting
- What the reader experiences: The chapter defines NPV as "present value of inflows minus present value of outflows" but never explains what present value is, why a dollar next year is worth less, or how a discount factor is computed. At line 261 the numbers 0.909 + 0.826 + 0.751 appear from nowhere as an "annuity factor". "Time value of money" (172), "cost of capital" (178), and "hurdle rate" (180) are used but not defined. Objective 4 and exercise F3 (compute NPV at 10 percent for a four-year stream) require exactly this; the solution uses 0.683 for year 4, which the chapter never shows how to get.
- Example: "The three-year annuity factor at 10 percent is 0.909 + 0.826 + 0.751 = 2.487."
- Fix: Add a 150–200 word box before "Payback period": one year's discount factor is 1 ÷ (1 + rate)^year; a small table of factors at 10 percent for years 1–4 (0.909, 0.826, 0.751, 0.683); one sentence each defining time value of money, cost of capital, and hurdle rate (the minimum return finance accepts); a three-line worked NPV. Then the line 261 arithmetic can refer back to it.

Similar at 45:221–223 and 396–398, 46:356–363, 47:432–435, 48:247–248 and 430–433, 49:361–363.

### P5 (medium) docs/parts/10-business-leadership/46-stakeholder-management-and-negotiation.md:383 vs 49-multi-year-transformation-communication-and-politics.md:388 — Contradictory advice on per-quarter incident targets

By Ch 46's own reasoning, a program truly averaging 10 a quarter would fail that gate about a third of the time by chance. A careful reader will wonder which chapter to believe. Ch 46 (414) also cites "the continuation proposal" that Ch 49 introduces, and in story time it is drafted in week 45, after Ch 46's weeks 41–44.
- Example (49:388): "Sev1+Sev2 at most 12 and 99.9% in each quarter".
- Fix: In Ch 49, either use Ch 46's rolling form or add one sentence explaining why a per-quarter mandatory threshold is acceptable at a gate, with a pointer to Ch 46. In Ch 46, at line 414, describe the prevention numbers without leaning on the "continuation proposal", or add "(see Chapter 49)".

## Patterns across your scope
- **Canon bookkeeping in reader prose** (all six case studies, two examples): see P3. Book-wide rule: a case opens with "Fictional; when; what you decide". Canon status lives outside the chapter.
- **Names without roles** (44, 45, 47, 48, 49): see P4. Rule: name plus role at first mention in each chapter, including recurring executives. Any person who does not change a decision is cut.
- **Arithmetic repeated in prose after a table** (44:265 and 297, 45:261, 46:221, 48:269 and 443–469, 49:216): the derivation sentences double the number load. Rule: a table or a single "check" line, not both. Long derivations go in a collapsible admonition or the solutions file.
- **Exercises that need canon from other chapters** (45 X1, 48 A1/A2, 49 A2): the solutions use facts such as named staff engineers, roadmap estimates, and CI decisions that are not in the chapter. Rule: an exercise states every fact its model answer uses, or links to the exact canon section.
- **Chapter-internal timeline jargon** ("week 34 forum", "week-44 proposal", "week 56 gate", "quarter 5"): rule: every week number in a case comes with a three-to-five word gloss of what happened then. State once per Part that quarters 5–8 are year 2.
- **Re-defining shared concepts**: escalation of commitment (45:162, 47:204, 49:83), Keil's status-reporting bias (47:147, 49:146), Fisher and Ury's interests and alternatives (46:158, 49:184). The re-definitions are short and mostly fine, but each chapter re-cites them as new. Rule: one-line reminder plus a link to the owning chapter.
- **Evidence-label vocabulary drift**: Ch 47 uses "this book's synthesis" (79) and "this book's reasoning" (115), which are not in `docs/references/evidence-labels.md`. Rule: use only the listed labels.
- **Diagram width**: 45:57 is `flowchart LR` with four parallel nodes, against the book's phone-width rule. Switch it to TD.

## Missing or thin content
- **Discounting primer** (Ch 45): see P1. Goes before "Payback period"; about 200 words plus a 4-row table. Also define churn and expansion (table at 87, needed for F1).
- **Sample executive proposal page one** (Ch 49): see P12. About 150 words in "Communicating to executives", plus about 80 words on running the decision meeting.
- **Running the director leadership team** (Ch 47, objective 5): the chapter offers a shared goal and "reallocation requests go to the director team meeting", but nothing on that meeting's agenda, cadence, norms for disagreement, or how the VP handles directors who undercut each other outside it. About 300 words in Deep Conceptual Explanation, after "Managing directors through goals".
- **Worked example of the layer decision** (Ch 48, objective 5): the "before adding a layer" content is a single list sentence (240–243) and a diagram, with no example. X2 asks the reader to apply it. A short Halvard or Northstar illustration (one coordination need, routed through the diagram) would take about 200 words.
- **Remote and hybrid work in location strategy** (Ch 48): Northstar has 12 remote US engineers, but the location section treats only sites and offshoring. A reader will expect a paragraph on remote hires: where it fits the "whole ownership" rule and how it interacts with the proximity finding at 136. About 150 words.
- **Discovery in practice** (Ch 44): discovery is defined and justified, but what a named discovery engineer does in a week (sessions attended, data pulled, the artifact produced) appears only as a four-item list. About 150 words, or a short "a discovery week" box.
- **Negotiating with external parties** (Ch 46): the chapter is entirely internal. One paragraph on how the floor and the five-part no apply when facing a customer or vendor directly (often through customer success) would complete the "stakeholder" promise. About 120 words.

## What works well (keep)
- Ch 44's corrected-order example (262–301): a random sample of 400 corrections changes three of the bets. It is the best single illustration in this scope of "information that changes the option set", and the guardrail on abandoned carts teaches metric gaming in one clause.
- Ch 44's date-origin table (224–231) and the "Where did this date come from?" question: simple, memorable, and immediately usable.
- Ch 45's "what the table really says" (267–278) and the case's "queueing versus swapping is noise" finding: they teach that the deciding skill is finding the input with swing, not computing the NPV.
- Ch 45's four channels (increase revenue, protect revenue, reduce costs, avoid costs): a shared vocabulary that later chapters can reuse.
- Ch 46's mental model and the self-contained payroll-export example (207–233): one generic company, a clear floor, and the interest uncovered ("data out before the first run"). It reads in two minutes and shows the whole method.
- Ch 46's five-part no and the Poisson table (388–392): the table makes a statistical point without equations and answers "why not just set a target" convincingly.
- Ch 47's role table by decisions owned (81–85) and the Halvard allocation example (243–295), which shows partition dependence in miniature with numbers a reader can check.
- Ch 48's stock-and-flow mental model and the externality test: two portable ideas, each stated in one sentence.
- Ch 49's split between reallocated capacity and new money (218–221), the transcript test (181–182), and the case's handling of Greg's private offer: concrete, ethical, and memorable.
- Every case ends with an explicit "the reader decides" list, and exercises are consistently framed as situations, not quizzes.
