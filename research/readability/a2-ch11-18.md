## Top problems (most impact on the reader first, at most 15)

### P1 (high) 16-flow-lead-time-dependencies-and-unplanned-work.md:71 — "Lead time" means two different things across the scope, unflagged
- What the reader experiences: Ch 14 (l.400) gives Customer Experience a "median lead time of six days". Ch 15 (l.382) says Payments and Integrations had "the shortest lead time of the product teams (4 days)". Both are commit-to-production figures from the baseline team table. Ch 16 then defines lead time as commitment-to-customer and shows a 76-day feature and medians of 11/24/41 days (l.422). The reader cannot tell why a team with 4-day lead times took 76 days, or which number Ch 18's "change lead time" refers to.
- Example: "Lead time here means elapsed time from when the organization commits to a piece of work to when customers can use it." (l.71)
- Fix: Use two fixed terms book-wide: "change lead time (commit to production)" and "delivery lead time (commitment to customer)". Qualify every use in Ch 14 and Ch 15. Add a two-row definition box at Ch 16 l.71 that names the baseline figures each term applies to.

### P2 (high) 13-decision-making-in-teams.md:141 — The concept section and the case study are built on canon from later chapters
- What the reader experiences: "The decision service level" is part of the Deep Conceptual Explanation, but it is taught through Ch 20's Northstar governance: three staff-engineer names with their domains, ten working days, and a two-quarter expiry. A reader on Ch 13 hasn't read Ch 20. The case study (l.398–442) then stacks week 4, tier 1, an exemption, a "compensating control", an incident lead from "the director's office", week 26, notification-svc moving, "Chapter 21's case funds a permissions extraction", and Chapter 28's dispute. The lesson, that the workaround removed the pressure to decide, is buried.
- Example: "Northstar's staff engineers run a decision rule that Chapter 20 describes as part of standards governance: each standards domain has one owner (Kwame Mensah for operational minimums and pipelines, Ines Carvalho for Core code, Li Na Zhou for data, migration, and configuration)…"
- Fix: Teach the service level generically: an owner per domain, a fixed response time, exemptions with an expiry. Move the Northstar names into the Engineering Organization Example. Open the case with a five-line "what you need to know" box: module, candidates, exemption, deadline. Define "compensating control". Drop the Ch 21 and Ch 28 forward references, or give the one fact each supplies.

### P3 (high) 14-distributed-teams-team-health-and-dysfunction.md:91 — Internal registry codes the reader can't resolve
- What the reader experiences: These codes look like references to something the reader should already know: "disputed claim D5" (Ch 14 l.91), "gap G6" (Ch 15 l.148), "research gap G7" (Ch 18 l.61), "Disputed claim D2" (Ch 18 l.132), "Disputed claim D1" (Ch 18 l.191, l.216). None is linked or explained.
- Example: "**The book's position on remote, hybrid, or colocated teams** (disputed claim D5): no study shows one arrangement is best…"
- Fix: Remove the codes from chapter prose. If the trail matters, write "(the book records this as a disputed claim; see Disputed claims)" with a link to a published page. Never use a bare code.

### P4 (medium) 18-measuring-engineering-productivity.md:84 — Notes on source provenance interrupt the teaching
- What the reader experiences: Several passages say how a source was accessed rather than what it says. Examples: "last updated January 5, 2026 and re-checked for this chapter" (l.84); SPACE's "full text could not be fetched… the dimension names come from two secondary pages" (l.139–145); DevEx's "full text was not readable either" (l.157); "read for this book from an archived copy" (l.173). The same habit appears in Ch 11 l.121–123, Ch 13 l.213–214 and l.225, and Ch 17 l.213–216 (Brooks "verified… only through a secondary source"). A practitioner reads these as hedging and loses the thread.
- Example: "Its full text could not be fetched for this book, so the dimension names come from two secondary pages…"
- Fix: Keep one evidence label per claim in the prose, for example "(industry research, survey-based)". Move access level and verification notes to the bibliography entry or a footnote.

### P5 (medium) 18-measuring-engineering-productivity.md:134 — SPACE is named, never explained
- What the reader experiences: Objective 2 is "Build a balanced measurement set… using SPACE and DevEx as checklists". The SPACE section spends its words on provenance. It lists the five dimension names but never says what "Performance" or "Communication and collaboration" mean, or which measure would represent each. The reader can't use it as a checklist.
- Example: "a post by Forsgren naming Satisfaction, Performance, Activity, Communication, and Efficiency"
- Fix: Add a five-row table: dimension, what it asks, one system measure, one survey question, and how it is gamed. This also gives Exercise A2 something to build on.

### P6 (medium) 17-predictability-and-managing-uncertainty.md:135 — Monte Carlo taught as a concept, with no recipe
- What the reader experiences: Objective 2 says "Build a delivery forecast… using… a Monte Carlo simulation", and F1 asks the reader to "run" one. The chapter shows a flow diagram and a results table but no way to produce them: no spreadsheet formula, no ten-line script, no tool pointer. A manager who finishes the chapter can't do it on Monday.
- Example: "Run, or reason through, a Monte Carlo forecast for the same backlog." (F1, l.448)
- Fix: Add a short box with a spreadsheet recipe (RANDBETWEEN over the history column, cumulative sum, MATCH, repeated across rows, then PERCENTILE) or equivalent pseudo-code. Note that free tools exist. About 150 words.

### P7 (medium) 16-flow-lead-time-dependencies-and-unplanned-work.md:15 — Forward references written as if already read; "release train" never defined
- What the reader experiences: "Chapter 23 decomposed the lead time of Northstar's Core release train" (l.15) and "Chapter 23 shows that a weekly train roughly halves the average wait" (l.256) read as recaps of a chapter seven chapters ahead. "Release train" itself is used in Ch 15 (l.255, l.370), Ch 16 (l.247 "Missed one train") and Ch 18 (l.252 "the Core train, which leaves twice a month") without a definition. The only definition is on the Northstar index page.
- Example: "Chapter 23 decomposed the lead time of Northstar's Core release train; this chapter follows a feature along its whole path."
- Fix: Define release train once, in Ch 15 at first use: "a fixed schedule on which all ready changes ship together; Core's left every two weeks". Rewrite the Ch 16 references in the future tense and state the needed fact inline ("a weekly train roughly halves the average wait for a release cut, as Chapter 23 shows").

### P8 (medium) 15-planning-prioritization-and-delivery-methods.md:428 — The case study's key numbers come from Ch 27's capacity model

## Patterns across your scope
- **Week and chapter references without a reminder.** Examples: "week 4 ownership decisions" (Ch 12 l.323), "the week 10 announcement" (Ch 14 l.395), "week 6 of the director's first quarter" (Ch 11 l.242), "delegation at level 4 in Chapter 2's scale" (Ch 13 l.249), "Chapter 3's hero loop" (Ch 12 l.349), "the transition described in Chapter 2" (Ch 11 l.14). Book-wide rule: every cross-reference carries a one-clause reminder of the fact it relies on, for example "(in week 4 the director assigned two unowned Core modules)".
- **Forward references phrased as recaps.** Ch 16 to Ch 23 (l.15, l.256), Ch 13 to Ch 20 (l.143) and Ch 21 (l.429), Ch 15 to Ch 27 (l.420, l.428) and Ch 42 (A2, l.470), Ch 18 to Ch 23 (l.104). Rule: a forward reference says "Chapter N will cover…" and states inline the one fact needed here.
- **Executives named without roles in exercises.** Helena Marsh (Ch 11 X1, Ch 13 X2, Ch 14 X1), Victor Lindqvist (Ch 11 X2, Ch 12 X2, Ch 13 X1), Greg Tanaka (Ch 12 A3, Ch 15 A2), Rajiv Menon (Ch 14 l.249), Li Na Zhou (Ch 12 l.399, Ch 16 l.409). Ch 15–17 already do it right ("Amara Okafor (CPO)", "Greg Tanaka, VP Sales"). Rule: name plus role at first mention in each chapter, exercises included, since readers jump to them.
- **Research sections written as chains of citations.** Ch 11 l.105–164 is four dense paragraphs of sample sizes and access caveats. Ch 12 l.83–97 and l.145–158, and Ch 13 l.191–227, are similar. Ch 14 l.77–83 shows the better form: a table of study, setting, finding and "does not show", followed by one paragraph of synthesis. Rule: summarize research in that table format and keep the prose for the takeaway.
- **Repetition across chapters.** Herbsleb and Mockus' 2.5× finding is explained in full in both Ch 14 l.79 and Ch 16 l.196. The survey-gaming loop is drawn in Ch 14 l.137–147 and again in Ch 18 l.63–73. Lencioni's pyramid is introduced in Ch 11, Ch 13 and Ch 14. Edmondson 1999 is re-summarized in Ch 12 l.134 and Ch 13 l.208 (brief; acceptable). Rule: the owning chapter explains a source once; others link with one line.
- **Inconsistent terms for the same thing.** "Change failure rate" (Ch 11, 14, 18 tables) against "change fail rate" (Ch 18 prose, DORA's term). "Lead time" in two senses (P1). "Unplanned share", "unplanned work" and "interrupts" are used interchangeably. Rule: one glossary term per concept, enforced by the validator if possible.
- **Pages that are mostly numbers.** Ch 15's CD3 example and Ch 17's Monte Carlo use numbers well: one table, one conclusion. Ch 16's case and Ch 14's travel and departure costs pile up figures. Rule: at most one numeric table per section, and no arithmetic in prose with more than three figures.

## Missing or thin content
- **Ch 12, knowledge sharing beyond knowledge risk.** The title promises knowledge sharing, but the chapter covers only single points of failure. Missing: onboarding as knowledge transfer, design docs and RFCs as a sharing channel, internal talks, guilds or communities of practice, and how a director spreads knowledge between teams, not only within one. Belongs in Deep Conceptual Explanation, about 500–700 words.
- **Ch 12, how to negotiate working agreements.** Objective 2 says "Negotiate". The how is one playbook line (step 4). Missing: running the session, turning complaints into observable agreements, and handling a member who objects. About 300 words.
- **Ch 14, teams spread across time zones and async-first work.** Northstar's sites are one hour apart, so the chapter never works through a real time-zone spread. One table row (l.261) covers under four hours of overlap. Missing: handoff protocols, overlap windows, async decision-making, follow-the-sun on-call, and remote onboarding of new hires (Emanuel et al.'s finding begs for this). About 500 words plus a small non-Northstar example.
- **Ch 15, the team roadmap cadence.** Objective 4 is delivered in ten lines (l.190–200). Missing: a worked now/next/later example for one team and what the monthly review looks at. About 300 words.
- **Ch 17, a Monte Carlo recipe and item right-sizing.** See P6. Also missing: how to slice items to a similar size so that throughput counts are meaningful (only "agree a rough slicing rule", l.393), and a short word on story points against item counts. About 300 words.
- **Ch 18, SPACE dimensions explained.** See P5. About 250 words as a table.
- **Ch 13, judging a decision by its process, not its outcome.** X2 asks whether "the decision process failed" after a bad outcome, but the chapter never separates decision quality from outcome quality. About 150–200 words in Deep Conceptual Explanation or Trade-offs.
- **A book glossary.** These terms recur without definitions in scope: Sev1/Sev2, tier 1/2/3, release train, FTE, change failure rate, unplanned work, prevention allocation, skip-level, opex. One generated page under References, linked from first uses. About one page.
- **Exercises that need canon the chapter doesn't give.** Ch 12 X1 (search-svc) is solved in the solutions with facts not in the chapter: tier 2, no runbook, partial alerts, INC-407. Ch 11 X2 needs the $185,000 loaded cost, which isn't in Ch 11. Either add the facts to the exercise stem or point to the exact baseline section.

## What works well (keep)
- Every chapter states its mental model in a blockquote near the top, with a loop diagram that shows a real mechanism. Ch 11's "two dials", Ch 16's "lead time is mostly waiting" and Ch 17's "a date is a distribution" are memorable and organize the rest of each chapter.
- Worked examples where the numbers carry the argument: Ch 15's volume order against economic order ($88k against $158k), Ch 16's standing-orders value-stream map, Ch 17's single-point plan against the simulation table, and Ch 18's per-engineer league table with Wilson intervals. Each ends in a clear "so what".
- Definitions in tables that a reader can reuse directly: Ch 13's four methods and five record fields, Ch 17's forecast/target/commitment table, Ch 18's metric, gaming and pair table, and Ch 14's study, finding and "does not show" table.
- Failure Modes consistently give a named failure, a signal and a countermeasure. They read as checklists a manager would actually use.
- Evidence is calibrated honestly (Ch 11 l.160–164, Ch 15 l.138–140, Ch 18 l.128–132): strong claims are separated from weak ones in plain words a CTO could repeat to a board.
- Most case studies end on an open decision tied to a specific exercise (Ch 11 A1, Ch 13 A1, Ch 18 X1), so the case drives the practice rather than decorating it.
- Exercises are concrete and, with the exceptions noted above, solvable from the chapter. The solutions name criteria and the typical weak answer.
