## Top problems (most impact on the reader first, at most 15)

### P1 (high) docs/parts/07-organization-design/36-reorganizations-evolution-and-succession.md:505 — The succession tally contradicts the table and the rule
- What the reader experiences: The rule (lines 271–278) says coverage needs a ready-now or interim cover, plus someone in development, and that neither may hold another critical role. In the table, the Gap column marks Payments as "One person in both columns" and Customer Experience as "Each covers half the team", so the reader reads both as gaps. Then the summary counts both as covered. The rule never says whether one person may fill both columns. F3 asks the reader to apply exactly this rule. The solution calls Orders and Payments "fragile even where the rule is met", a fourth position.
- Example: "By the coverage rule only Orders, Payments and Integrations, and Customer Experience are covered, two of them by one person in both columns." (line 524)
- Fix: Add a fourth condition to the rule: "the two are different people", or say explicitly that one person may fill both columns. Then make the Gap column and the summary agree, for example: "covered on paper; one person in both columns." Align the F3 solution with the rule.

### P2 (medium) docs/parts/06-technical-leadership/29-staff-and-principal-engineers.md:301 — Northstar program names used as if already known (also Ch 30, 31, 33)
- What the reader experiences: Several chapters refer to "the quality program", "the funded permissions extraction" and "the release working group" without a clause saying what they are. The year in brief mentions only "the shared permissions module ... deferred". A reader who skipped Chapters 20–27 cannot tell what is being extracted, who funded it, or what the program is.
- Example: "None has a scope that outlasts the quality program." (Ch 29:301); "In quarter 2 Ines leads the funded permissions extraction." (Ch 29:426)
- Fix: At first mention in each chapter, add a gloss of about eight words, for example "the funded permissions extraction (moving permission checks out of core/customer into their own module, led by Ines)" and "the quality program (the director's year-1 reliability mandate)". Ch 29:426, Ch 30:497, Ch 31:280 and Ch 33:255 need it. Ch 32:205–221 already explains it well and can serve as the reference.

## Patterns across your scope
- **Northstar shorthand without a gloss**: program names, week-numbered events and incident IDs recur across Ch 29–36, sometimes glossed and sometimes not (P2, P6). Book-wide rule: every named program, initiative or incident gets a gloss of 6–12 words at first mention in each chapter, whatever earlier chapters did.
- **Arithmetic in prose inside examples and cases**: Ch 30:279–285 and 436–438, Ch 32:273–286 and 450–463, Ch 34:425–428, Ch 35:466–469. Ch 33:240–250 and Ch 36:287–310 show the better form. Rule: more than two numbers combined in one sentence moves into a table, and the sentence states the conclusion.
- **Exercises reaching outside the chapter**: Ch 28 F1, Ch 31 F3 and Ch 33 F1 point to `components.csv`, the baseline page or the year-1 record. Some solutions use day-90 facts the chapter never gives. Rule: any data an exercise needs appears in the exercise or the chapter. Data-file links are optional enrichment only.
- **Long ownership preambles**: most openers end with three or four "Chapter N owns X" clauses before the reader knows the chapter's point (Ch 29:10–13, Ch 31:9–11, Ch 32:10–12). Rule: one sentence of cross-references in the opener. Put the rest where each topic first comes up.
- **Studies stacked before the point**: Ch 29 (conflict and feedback research), Ch 31:99–107 (four migration studies), Ch 33:88–105 (mirroring). The labels are honest and useful, but the conclusion comes last. Rule: state the takeaway sentence first, then the studies, at most three per paragraph.
- **Per-chapter re-definitions**: dependency link (Ch 33, 34, 35 twice), truck factor (Ch 29, 30, 36), contract test (Ch 28, 31, 34). These help the stand-alone reader and are acceptable. Keep them to one clause and use identical wording.

## Missing or thin content
- **Ch 35, product portfolio input**: the chapter says a design has three inputs and says to "write the portfolio as a short table", but the Northstar case never shows Northstar's portfolio (products, expected life, direction, release mechanics). The reader cannot check the proposal against the input the chapter calls essential. Add a four-to-six-row portfolio table before the proposal (about 120 words).
- **Ch 36, the person who wanted the role and did not get it**: the chapter covers the manager whose scope shrinks, but not the internal candidate passed over, which A1 raises ("one mobile engineer had told Daniel they also wanted to lead"). Add a short paragraph to "Finishing in weeks" (about 100 words).
- **Ch 29, choosing and onboarding a person into a staff scope**: scoping is thorough, but how to choose between an internal candidate and an external hire, and the first month in a new scope, is thin. It appears only indirectly in X1. Add a short subsection or a playbook step (about 150 words).
- **Ch 34, dotted-line and matrix reporting**: the playbook says to list "dotted lines", but the chapter never says how to count them in span or when they help. Add 2–3 sentences to "Why there is no universal span".
- **Ch 32, retiring a platform capability**: switch-off dates are covered for legacy systems but not for platform features nobody adopted. A sentence or two under Failure Modes would close the loop with "talk to non-adopters".

## What works well (keep)
- Every chapter states its mental model in the first screen and comes back to it in the decision framework. A reader could explain the main idea of each chapter afterwards.
- Evidence labels are candid and specific: "no study compares these arrangements" (Ch 28), "an analogy with no validated instrument" (Ch 33), "this book does not rely on consulting-firm surveys" (Ch 36). They build trust.
- Decision frameworks are tables and diagrams the reader can use directly: Ch 28's decision-class table, Ch 31's extraction path, Ch 33's symptom-to-move table, Ch 36's readiness gates.
- Transition cost and gaming are named for nearly every recommendation, in line with the book's voice rules.
- Worked examples outside Northstar (Ch 28's 120-engineer board queue, Ch 35's four companies, Ch 36's 60-engineer reorganization) teach the arithmetic without case baggage.
- The case studies end on open decisions with real tension (Victor's hallway remark in Ch 28, Ines's critic pattern in Ch 29, Rafael's extraction push in Ch 31). Most exercises give their own data (Ch 33 A1, Ch 35 F2, Ch 34 F3).
- The "Northstar so far" boxes are consistent and short, and they orient a reader who arrives cold.
- Ch 32's five-step strangler slice and Ch 31's five-step database split are concrete enough to act on next week.
