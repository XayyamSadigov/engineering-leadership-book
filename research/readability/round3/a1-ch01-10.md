## Top problems (most impact on the reader first, at most 15)

### P1 (medium) docs/parts/02-people-management/10-motivation-retention-and-senior-engineers.md:422 — Case Study overloaded with threads
- What the reader experiences: The case covers five things at once: the eight exit records, the ledger engineer who left, Ines's stay interview, her review behavior (the "hostage" thread), and Tomasz's offer. It also brings in a funded "permissions extraction" slice of core/customer and her first-quarter mandate. It ends with four separate decisions. A reader who skipped Chapters 1–9 cannot tell which thread carries the chapter's lesson. The Ines material also needs facts from the year 1 record (her mandate, contract tests, the Core code standard).
- Example: "Her full redesign of core/customer, a module four teams change, is not funded; a first slice, extracting permissions, is funded for quarter 2, and she leads it." (line 459)
- Fix: Split the case. Keep the retention case (the exit records, the ledger engineer who left, Tomasz) here. Move the Ines stay interview and the hostage dynamic into a short second case under "Managing engineers more expert than you", or cut them to three lines. Cut the funding detail about the permissions slice. Reduce the closing decisions to two.

### P2 (medium) docs/parts/02-people-management/05-one-on-ones-coaching-and-mentorship.md:269 — Worked example uses data the reader has not yet seen, in two heavy tables
- What the reader experiences: The Engineering Organization Example quotes the Case Study's six-week sample ("84 meetings, news in 11") before the Case Study appears. It then gives two arithmetic tables, the second in "person-hours per item". One sentence is hard to parse even after rereading.
- Example: "Under D, people keep all or three quarters of their face time, and Daniel recovers about 2.7 hours a week." (line 284)
- Fix: Keep only the cadence table (A–D) in this section. Move the "news per person-hour" comparison into the Case Study, after the coded-sample table. Rewrite line 284 as: "Under D, the two leads keep all their face time, and the other 13 keep about three quarters of it. Daniel recovers about 2.7 hours a week."

### P3 (medium) multiple chapters — Source-access bookkeeping leaks into reader text
- What the reader experiences: Repeated notes on which part of a source was read ("abstract", "publisher's summary", "read in full", "does not reproduce"). They read as the author apologizing for not reading the source, which undermines confidence in the findings that follow. They also lengthen sentences that are already dense.
- Example: "Their summaries do not single out predictors that hold everywhere, so this chapter does not list any." (ch10:76). "This book does not reproduce the four paths." (ch10:85)
- Other instances: ch01:228–236 ("Lazear, Shaw, and Stanton's abstract reports…", "Hoffman and Tadelis's working-paper abstract reports…"); ch02:85 ("Her publisher describes…"); ch03:176 ("Its abstract reports…"); ch05:93 ("the summary does not describe samples or methods"); ch07:86 ("Its abstract reports…"); ch08:604 ("read in full"); and in the ch01 F3 solution ("the chapter reports these studies from their abstracts").
- Fix: State the finding with its evidence label and scope ("one firm", "survey data"), and drop how the source was accessed. Where the limit matters, express it as a weaker label. For ch10:85, either name the four paths in one line or drop the sentence.

### P4 (medium) docs/parts/02-people-management/10-motivation-retention-and-senior-engineers.md:189 — Expert-management section is disconnected from the mental model
- What the reader experiences: The mental model is about pressure, an event, an alternative, and what binds people. The motivation (line 107) and expert (line 189) sections never refer back to it. The chapter reads as three essays: retention, motivation, managing experts. Clarity drops to "I could explain the retention part".
- Example: "Most managers manage people who know their systems better. The mistake is to treat this as a contest to win or concede." (line 191)
- Fix: Open each section with one bridging sentence. For example: "Unmet autonomy, competence, and relatedness are the most common sources of accumulated pressure." And: "For senior experts, the strongest binding is a mandate and decisions that hold; their strongest pressure is advice taken selectively." Add the three needs as rows under "Accumulated pressure" in the element table.

## Patterns across your scope
- **Source-access notes in prose** (P3): ch01, ch02, ch03, ch05, ch07, ch08, ch10. Book-wide rule: state the claim, its scope, and its label. Never say which part of the source was read.
- **Arithmetic in prose and stacked figures**: ch03:147 and 252; ch05:284–291; ch08:329–332; ch09:308–311; ch10:278–290. Most chapters already use tables well. Rule: no more than two numbers per sentence, and anything derived goes in a table row or the solutions.
- **Exercises and solutions that rely on the year 1 record**: ch03 F3/X1, ch06 A2, ch09 A2, ch10 F3. Rule: an exercise's own text must contain every Northstar fact the model answer depends on, beyond the overview and cast.
- **Cross-chapter re-explanation**: Grove's managerial-output definition is re-introduced in ch01, ch02, ch03, and ch04. The manager-effect studies (Hoffman and Tadelis, Project Oxygen) are re-summarized in ch01, ch04, and ch10. The Peter Principle is explained in ch02 and ch04. The "mandate-free staff engineer" appears in ch01, ch03, ch04, and ch10. Most instances already point back, which is good, but each still spends two to four sentences re-explaining. Rule: one sentence plus a link after the owning chapter.
- **Unglossed Northstar internals**: module paths (core/customer, core/order-capture), the release working group, release numbers, INC IDs with partial descriptions. Rule: every internal name gets a five-word gloss at first use in each chapter, or is cut.
- **Evidence-label density**: ch08 and ch10 carry six or seven "this book's recommendation" tags, sometimes two per section. Rule: label at the section or table head when the whole block shares a label.

## Missing or thin content
- **Ch 4, senior manager and manager-of-managers rung**: it appears in the ladder table and once in the Enterprise Application, with no treatment of how the job differs from director. Needs one paragraph in "What changes at each step".
- **Ch 4, measuring staff engineers**: managers get a four-area evidence table, but staff-plus engineers, who sit on the same map, get none. One short table or a pointer to Chapter 29 with two lines of substance would do.
- **Ch 8, the pay conversation itself**: the chapter covers deciding pay, but not how to tell an engineer about a raise, a below-band position, or an equity adjustment. That conversation is what the reader will actually hold. About 200 words, alongside the "not yet" section.
- **Ch 9, problems during the first 90 days or probation**: Chapter 7 ends with onboarding, and Chapter 9 assumes an established employee. A new hire who is not working out (probation, an early exit) is a common case. One short subsection.
- **Ch 10, senior engineers who are not experts-in-residence**: the title promises "managing senior engineers", but the section covers only highly expert or opinionated people. It does not cover the plateaued senior engineer or the senior who wants scope the team cannot offer. About 300 words, linking to Chapter 8's career conversation.
- **Ch 5, confidential disclosures in one-on-ones**: what to do when a report discloses harassment, health, or a legal matter. Chapter 6 touches on this in one sentence. Two or three lines in "Three situations the design must handle".

## What works well (keep)
- **Every chapter states its mental model in a blockquote early, with a mechanism diagram.** Chapters 2, 5, and 6 are models of the form: the loops in Ch 2 (engineer's loop versus manager's loop) and Ch 5 (healthy versus decay) stick.
- **Honest evidence handling**: the effect-size limits, the "not software teams" caveats, the contested task-conflict findings (Ch 6), and the 2022 validity revision (Ch 7). This treats the reader as an adult, and the "what .42 buys" translation is excellent.
- **Concrete, reusable tools**: the five-level delegation scale (Ch 2), the leverage table (Ch 3), the decay-signal table (Ch 5), the five-part "not yet" (Ch 8), the diagnosis order (Ch 9), and counter-offer pricing (Ch 10).
- **Each metric comes with how it is gamed**, consistently, without sounding formulaic.
- **Case studies that end at a decision rather than a moral** (Priya in Ch 2, Tamsin in Ch 7, Bram in Ch 9). The Tamsin case in particular ("a capable engineer hired against the wrong measure") is a model of illustrating without blaming.
- **The annotated difficult-conversation dialogue** (Ch 6) is the single most useful passage for a first-time manager in this scope.
- **Exercises** are situational and mostly solvable from the chapter. The sampled solutions show reasoning, criteria, and the weak answer, as promised.
