## Top problems (most impact on the reader first, at most 15)

### P1 (medium) docs/parts/05-engineering-quality/24-reliability-slos-and-operational-readiness.md:537 — Exercises that need the incident CSV, not the chapter
- What the reader experiences: Ch 24 F2 asks the reader to take "the 16 incidents that monitoring or an engineer detected". The chapter lists only the 14 customer-detected ones, so the reader has nothing to work from. The solution relies on INC-402, 407, 413, 420, and 423, which appear nowhere in the chapter. The same problem shows up more mildly in Ch 25 F3 (25:524: "`trigger` is `monolith-release`", and the chapter's note never marks which of the 19 incidents rode the train), in Ch 26 F3 (26:563: the per-CI-system tier counts are only in `components.csv`), and in Ch 27 F1 (27:532: classify all 30 from the CSV). The book's own rule says exercises must be solvable from the chapter plus the overview, cast, timeline, and quarter 4 briefing.
- Example: "Using `incidents-last-quarter.csv`, take the 16 incidents that monitoring or an engineer detected."
- Fix: In Ch 24, add a collapsible note listing the 16 with one-line descriptions, mirroring the existing note of 14. In Ch 25, tag the train incidents in the "19 incidents" note (for example "(train)"). In Ch 26, add a four-row table (CI system, components, of which tier 1) to the example. In Ch 27, reword F1 to "using the figures in this chapter", with the CSV as optional depth.

### P2 (medium) docs/parts/05-engineering-quality/24-reliability-slos-and-operational-readiness.md:163 — Burn-rate alerting is introduced too densely
- What the reader experiences: One paragraph introduces precision, recall, detection time, reset time, burn rate, and multiwindow alerting, with a "twelfth as long" short window. The four evaluation criteria are never used again. The 14.4 derivation comes after the table, so the table's numbers look arbitrary on first reading. This is the chapter's main operational technique, and a reader new to SRE stalls here.
- Example: "Its starting point for a 99.9 percent SLO over 30 days uses **multiwindow** alerts: each long window is confirmed over a short window about a twelfth as long, so the alert stops soon after the problem does."
- Fix: Cut the four criteria, or reduce them to one clause. Define burn rate with a one-line example ("burn rate 2 means the 30-day budget would be gone in 15 days"). Put the 14.4 derivation before the table. Explain multiwindow in a single sentence of plain consequence: "the long window proves it is serious, the short one proves it is still happening".

### P3 (medium) docs/parts/05-engineering-quality/21-technical-debt.md:236 — Two engineer-time rates in one assumption
- What the reader experiences: The reader has just accepted $185,000 a year in Chapter 20. Here they meet $800 a day, plus a sentence comparing it with $3,558 a week, which appears nowhere else in the chapter. The comparison raises the question it tries to answer: why is this chapter using a different rate?
- Example: "It is higher per day than the book's planning rate of $3,558 per engineer-week because Finance counts only working days."
- Fix: Drop the comparison sentence. Write "An engineer-day costs about $800 ($185,000 a year over about 230 working days; see [engineer-time rate](glossary))". Better still, use the one planning rate throughout Part V, as Chapters 20, 22, and 27 already do.

### P7 (medium) docs/parts/05-engineering-quality/23-code-review-ci-cd-and-release-engineering.md:170 — Red-main reasoning puts the conclusion last and the logic in a footnote
- What the reader experiences: The reader has to compare an "upper bound on false reds" with "measured red time" and work out why one exceeding the other implies noise. Most will skim past without getting the point: Core's red main is mostly pipeline noise, not broken changes.
- Example: "An **illustrative** upper bound on false reds (about 10 hours a week, see the note) is larger than the measured red time itself. So most of the 22 percent is probably pipeline noise rather than broken changes."

## Patterns across your scope
- **The same Northstar beats retold chapter after chapter.** The week 9 repeat of INC-427 is told in Ch 21, 22, 23, 25, and 27 (Ch 23 and 25 both describe the failed rollback in detail). The 12 FTE split (Billing 25 percent, 10 percent, 15 percent) appears in Ch 21, 22, 27, and the info boxes. "3 of 15 tier 1 components meet the minimums, 8 by day 90" appears in Ch 19, 20, and 24. The QA week 10 announcement appears in Ch 19 and twice in Ch 27. Each retelling is short, but a reader going straight through feels déjà vu. Rule: one chapter owns each event (the week 9 Sev1 is Ch 25, the allocation is Ch 27). Other chapters give one clause plus a link, never the mechanism again.
- **A fresh Sev1/Sev2 definition in every chapter.** A 30–40-word parenthetical defining Sev1 and Sev2 appears in Ch 19, 21, 23, 25, 26, and 27, plus the info boxes. The rule to define at first use per chapter is sound, but the wording is long. Rule: a short standard form ("Sev1 and Sev2, the two highest [severity](glossary) ratings") and let the glossary carry the full definition.
- **Exercises that depend on CSV files.** These are Ch 24 F2, 25 F3, 26 F3, 27 F1, and partly 23 F2. Rule: every number an exercise needs is in the chapter, a collapsible note, or the four allowed Northstar pages; CSVs are optional depth.
- **Evidence-label sentences in every paragraph.** Almost every research paragraph ends with "This is documented practice at one company, not ..." or "That is a research finding from ...". Each is fine alone; together they make the Deep Conceptual sections feel defensive and slow (worst in Ch 19:182–211, Ch 22:94–151, and Ch 23:116–131). Rule: fold the label into the sentence that introduces the source ("Google's account, documented practice, reports ..."), and keep standalone caveat sentences for claims a reader might over-apply.
- **Arithmetic notes work when used, and hurt when skipped.** Ch 22, 23, and 24 move calculations into `??? note` blocks and stay readable; Ch 20:18–20, 21:259–263, and 26:341–346 leave them in prose or main tables. Rule: any calculation with more than two operations goes in a note.
- **Forward and backward pointers without a one-line reminder.** Most are done well (INC numbers are always glossed). The exceptions are the weekly train in Ch 22, "Project 8" in Ch 27:274 (never explained), and "Chapter 24's readiness bar" in Ch 20:322 before the reader has met it. Rule: every cross-reference carries a clause saying what is there.

## What works well (keep)
- **Every chapter states its mental model in a blockquote and then builds on it.** Examples: "Deploying is moving code; releasing is exposing behavior" (Ch 23), "Debt is defined by its interest, not by its principal" (Ch 21), and "Shut off the inflow before you drain the stock" (Ch 27). A reader could explain each chapter's main idea afterwards.
- **Decision frameworks are short diagrams plus tables with "fits when / fails when / early warning" columns.** These answer "so what do I do?" directly. Ch 19, Ch 20 (the enforcement ladder), and Ch 24 (the error-budget stance table) are especially good.
- **The case studies say what went wrong.** Examples: the Jenkins check reverted on day one (Ch 20), Customer Experience's unregistered upgrade (Ch 21), the slipped action before the week 9 Sev1 (Ch 25), and the week 10 QA announcement (Ch 27). This makes the advice credible and teaches the failure modes concretely.
- **Day-90 results are reported with honest uncertainty.** "One contract catch is an anecdote" (Ch 22) and "consistent with improvement without proving it" (Ch 27) model the measurement discipline the part teaches.
- **INC numbers always come with a few words on what happened,** and Ch 23 gathers its ten in a collapsible table. The incident data stays usable without the reader memorizing it.
- **The lead-time decomposition table (Ch 23:288) and the capacity model (Ch 27:247)** are the best quantitative exhibits in the part. Each makes a single argument with a single table.
- **Clear ownership boundaries between chapters** (19 for quality definitions, 22 for tests, 23 for the pipeline, 24 for SLOs, 25 for incidents) mean topics are cross-referenced rather than re-taught. The exception is the Northstar story beats noted above.
