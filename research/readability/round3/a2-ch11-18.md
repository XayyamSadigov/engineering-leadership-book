## Top problems (most impact on the reader first, at most 15)

### P1 (high) 04-execution/15-planning-prioritization-and-delivery-methods.md:427 and :468 — capacity arithmetic in prose, built on a later chapter's model
- What the reader experiences: two dense paragraphs of numbers. Lines 427–439 give 31 candidates, 17 items, 560 engineer-weeks, 780, a 25 percent allowance, 34 percent and 686. Lines 468–472 give 46 FTE, 28 percent, 22.4 FTE and 12 FTE. The reader is mixing headcount, FTE and engineer-weeks, and is told the model is built in Chapter 27. They cannot see how 80 became 46, or how much is left after the renewals. The lesson ("effort fits, calendar does not"; "plan against measured capacity") gets lost in the sums.
- Example: "The director's capacity model (Chapter 27 builds it) projects about 46 FTE of feature capacity for quarter 2, not 80 engineers. It subtracts two things."
- Fix: replace both paragraphs with two small tables.
  - (a) Baseline plan: assumed capacity 780, measured 686, committed 560, delivered 7 of 17, with one line on why calendar time differs from effort.
  - (b) Quarter 2: headcount 80, minus unplanned (28%) 22.4, minus prevention 12, gives about 46 for features, minus renewal staffing 11, gives about 35 to rank.
  - Add one sentence on what an FTE is, and drop "Chapter 27 builds it" from the sentence.

### P2 (medium) 04-execution/16-flow-lead-time-dependencies-and-unplanned-work.md:286 — value-stream arithmetic and predictions packed into prose
- What the reader experiences: right after a clear map and table, the reader has to do sums in their head. Flow efficiency 23 ÷ 76, waits of 25 of 53, then "about a quarter", "another quarter", "the rest", then 76 to 71 days, 6 percent, and 16 days. The punchline (fixing cross-team queues beats a faster train three to one) is buried.
- Example: "Here the 9-day train wait might become 4 or 5, so lead time falls from 76 to about 71 days, roughly 6 percent. Had Billing planned the invoicing change in the same cycle as Orders, its 14-day wait could have been 2 or 3 days"
- Fix: add a "waits by kind" row to the table: intake 12, other teams 25, release 13, review 3. Then add a three-row "change, days saved" table: weekly train about 5, Billing in the same cycle about 11, self-service environment about 5. State the conclusion in one sentence.

### P3 (medium) Ch 11–18, e.g. 03-high-performing-teams/11-high-performance-trust-safety-and-accountability.md:376, 12-…:229, 04-execution/15-…:405, 18-…:263 — "Core" is never defined
- What the reader experiences: "Core", "core/order-capture", "the Core release train" and "Core questions" appear in seven of the eight chapters. Only Chapter 13 (line 411) says it is a monolith. A reader who enters at Chapter 15 or 18 has no idea why Orders and Billing share a release train, and that idea carries the argument of Chapter 18's "the ranking measures architecture".
- Example: "two senior engineers took over review of core/order-capture (the Core module that takes orders)" (Ch 11:376).
- Fix: at first mention in each chapter, write "Core (Northstar's shared Java monolith, released as one unit; Orders and Billing make most of its changes)". Link it once to `glossary.md#core`.

### P4 (medium) 04-execution/18-measuring-engineering-productivity.md:126 — the book's position on speed against stability is unreadable
- What the reader experiences: after a careful paragraph, the reader reaches a sentence that seems to say "contested" is itself the position. They finish unsure what to believe or say.
- Example: "The evidence on this is contested, and that is the book's position."
- Fix: "This book's position: across organizations, speed and stability tend to go together, so the trade-off is not a law. Whether deploying more often lowers *your* failure rate is a hypothesis to test in your own data."

### P5 (medium) 04-execution/18-measuring-engineering-productivity.md:260 — an unexplained "95 percent interval" carries a key argument
- What the reader experiences: the "Plausible range" column and the claim that "every team's interval overlaps" decide whether teams can be ranked. A reader without statistics does not know what the range means, or why overlap means "cannot rank". "(24 to 78 per team)" does not say what is being counted.
- Example: "The plausible range is a standard 95 percent interval for a rate built from one quarter's small number of deployments (24 to 78 per team)."
- Fix: replace with a plain explanation. "With only 24 to 78 deployments in a quarter, a team's true failure rate could reasonably sit anywhere in this range. When two teams' ranges overlap, one quarter cannot tell you which is really worse." Link to an evidence-glossary entry if one is added.

## Patterns across your scope
- **Arithmetic in prose in Northstar passages.** Occurs in Ch 15:427 and :468, Ch 16:286, Ch 13:248, Ch 11:292 (the 10.5 hours against 2.2 hours paragraph) and Ch 17:300. The non-Northstar tables (Ch 15 CD3, Ch 14 travel cost) show the book can do this well. Book-wide rule: any passage with three or more derived numbers becomes a table with an "assumption" column, and the prose keeps only the conclusion.
- **Northstar shorthand without a one-line gloss.** Core, the release train, "week 4/5/10" events, core/customer and core/scheduler, the 72-hour failure rule. The "Northstar so far" boxes help, but they cover the setting, not the system. Rule: each chapter glosses Core and any module name at first use, and every "in week N" event carries five words of what happened.
- **Capacity units shift between chapters.** The same idea appears as FTE (Ch 12, 15, 16, 18), engineer-weeks (Ch 15), "points of capacity" (Ch 12:421) and "percentage points" (Ch 16:450), and as headcount against FTE (Ch 15:469). Rule: use percent of capacity for shares and FTE for absolute amounts. Write "percentage points of capacity" in full the first time in each chapter.
- **Glossary links are missing where they matter most.** The glossary has entries for fully loaded cost, contract test, Core, ARR and Little's law, but the chapters that use those terms do not link them. Chapters link the delivery terms (lead time, CFR) diligently, but not the finance and Northstar-shorthand terms. Rule: run a link check for every glossary headword's first appearance per chapter.
- **Length runs over the target.** All eight chapters run 5,300–5,800 words, above the 2,500–5,000 aim. The excess is mostly research-table cells and second Northstar passages, not padding. The cost is reading time, not confusion. Rule: trim the "What the research measured" tables to one short sentence per cell, and keep one Northstar passage per chapter where two exist.
- **Repeated re-explanations across chapters.** Edmondson (1999) is re-explained in Ch 11, 12 and 13, and hidden profiles in Ch 11, 13 and 17, each in a sentence or two. The repetition is mild and acceptable. Rule: after the owning chapter, use a pointer only ("hidden profiles, Chapter 13").

## What works well (keep)
- **Research tables with a "Does not show" column** (Ch 11:103, Ch 14:72). They teach the reader to calibrate evidence without lecturing.
- **Non-Northstar illustrative examples** carry the main teaching while Northstar carries the case. Examples: Ch 13's decision log, Ch 15's outcome roadmap, and Ch 14's Chicago–Kraków team. This keeps the Northstar load down.
- **The "What you need to know for this case" box** in Ch 13:410. It is the best device in scope for readers who skipped earlier chapters, and should be copied to the Ch 15 and Ch 16 cases.
- **Ch 17's spreadsheet Monte Carlo recipe and forecast/target/commitment table.** Immediately usable.
- **Ch 18's gaming-and-pair table and the illustrative CFO answer.** They turn a contested topic into something a director can say on Monday.
- **Ch 16's explicit two-sense table for "lead time".** It resolves a terminology trap the rest of the book depends on.
- **Decision diagrams that are real mechanisms** (Ch 11 diagnosis tree, Ch 12 single-person-area tree, Ch 16 dependency tree). All are short and readable at phone width.
- **Solutions.** They show reasoning, give defensible alternatives and name the weak answer (for example ch12 X1, ch15 F2 with its double-counting warning). The ones sampled are solvable from their chapters, except ch16 A3.
