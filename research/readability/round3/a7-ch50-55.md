## Top problems (most impact on the reader first, at most 15)

### P1 (medium) 52-technology-investment-budgeting-and-financial-literacy.md:338, :564 — Four rates for one engineer's cost
- What the reader experiences: In one chapter the reader meets $185,000 per engineer-year, $157,250 capitalized per FTE-year, $800 per engineer-day (Finance's rate), and the book's $3,558 per engineer-week. The day rate and the week rate disagree ($3,558 ÷ 5 = $712), and the explanation comes inside a case-study sentence. A finance novice starts doubting every figure.
- Example: "Costs here use **Finance's day rate** of $800 per engineer-day ($185,000 ÷ about 230 working days). It is higher per day than the book's planning rate of $3,558 per engineer-week because Finance counts only working days."
- Fix: Add one row per rate to the "Terms used in this chapter" box: what it is, who uses it, and why it differs. Then refer to rates by name ("Finance's day rate") in the case. Better still, express the case-study scenarios in engineer-weeks at the book rate and show Finance's figure once, as a side note.

### P2 (medium) 53-risk-governance-regulation-and-security-strategy.md:379 — ER-01 exposure mixes two different quantities
- What the reader experiences: The exposure of losing one customer ($842,000 of margin) is added to a company-wide credits range at two quarters' rates. The reader cannot tell why all customers' credits belong in one customer's loss, or what the "$240,000 to $380,000" range means.
- Example: "At the 72 percent gross margin that is about $842,000 a year, plus $240,000 to $380,000 a year of credits at quarter 3's and quarter 2's rates."
- Fix: Split it into two bullets: "loss of one top-40 customer: about $842,000 a year of gross margin", and "credits company-wide while availability stays below contract: $240,000 to $380,000 a year". Say that the second applies whether or not anyone leaves. The derivation is already in the solutions file, so link it.

### P4 (medium) 51-enterprise-technology-strategy.md:224 — Allocation assumptions are written in Northstar shorthand

## Patterns across your scope
- **Open year-2 decisions carried as shorthand**: all six chapters reference the continuation, ADR-0001, the year-2 organization, the VP question, the workforce plan and the week 50 board. Chapters 52, 53 and 55 gloss "the continuation" in a clause each time; Chapter 51's assumptions and Chapter 54's case study do not gloss the reorganization. Recommended rule: every open Northstar decision named in a chapter gets a one-clause gloss at first mention, from a shared list of about six standard glosses.
- **ID-heavy tables** (TR-01..09, ER-01..09, ADR-0001, auth-svc, tax-svc, search-svc): concentrated in Chapter 53 and the Chapter 55 diligence table. Recommended rule: an ID appears with a three-to-six-word description at first mention in each chapter (Chapter 55 does this well for ADR-0001 and auth-svc). Use bare IDs only inside a table that already shows the description.
- **Arithmetic in prose despite the readability brief**: Chapter 52 (credits, envelope), Chapter 53 (ER-01, questionnaire hours), Chapter 54 (backfills and licenses at line 387). Recommended rule: a sentence states a result; operands go in a table column or the solutions.
- **Multiple cost rates**: $185,000 per year, $3,558 per week, $800 per day, $157,250 capitalized. Recommended rule: one rate per chapter. Any other rate gets a named entry in that chapter's terms box.
- **Exercises carry their own fact sheets**: this makes them solvable, but some (51 A2, 55 F3) read as status dumps. Recommended rule: an exercise gives only the facts its answer uses, with any number the chapter has not shown flagged as new.
- **Repeated definitions** (operating plan, budget envelope, board pack, audit committee): redefined in 50, 51, 52, 53 and 55. This is intentional for standalone reading and costs little; keep each to one parenthesis.

## Missing or thin content
- **Privacy and data-protection obligations (Ch 53)**: explicitly excluded, but a CTO reader will expect at least the engineering side: a data map, deletion and retention, processor agreements with vendors, and breach notification as a trigger in the incident-to-disclosure path. Add about 200 words in "Regulation and contracts through engineering controls", framed as engineering controls, with the jurisdictional detail left to counsel.
- **Cloud cost as a CTO lever (Ch 52)**: FinOps gets one bullet and one failure mode. A short worked example of unit cost (hosting per order or per customer) and how it reaches gross margin would connect to Chapter 54's inference-cost paragraph. About 250 words in the Engineering Organization Example or the Enterprise Application section.
- **In the room (Ch 55)**: preparation is covered well, but live behavior is not: answering a question you cannot answer, handling a director who pushes into management, and what to send after the meeting. About 150 words, or a short playbook step.
- **Trial design basics (Ch 54)**: exercise A3 asks for "the smallest effect your design could plausibly detect". The chapter says only that six teams can detect "a large effect". Two or three sentences on why few units mean low power would make A3 fairly solvable.

## What works well (keep)
- Every chapter opens with a quotable mental model in a blockquote, and the Deep Conceptual Explanation keeps returning to it. Chapter 50's "the role is a residual" and Chapter 54's "AI is an amplifier" are memorable and teachable.
- Evidence labeling is careful and useful to this reader. Chapter 54's study table, with setting, outcome and result, plus a plain explanation of standard error, is a model for presenting contested research.
- Chapter 52's terms box and its "The lesson:" lead-ins before each financial table make a finance-heavy chapter accessible to engineers.
- Decision diagrams are small, phone-readable, and explained in a table beneath (Ch 50's "does it stay with the CTO?", Ch 51's option decision point, Ch 55's diligence finding).
- Case studies end open, with "the reader decides", and the exercises pick up exactly those decisions. In the sampled solutions, exercises were solvable from the chapter plus the cast and overview. They state criteria and the weak answer clearly.
- Chapter 55's Poisson headline table turns an abstract point (thresholds near the average fail by chance) into something a reader can use in their own board deck.
- Chapter 50's sustainability subsection and first-90-days table address what a new CTO actually needs.
